\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Objective:} Graph Neural Network (GNN) papers predicting microRNA--target
interactions routinely report AUROC in the 0.91--0.99 range without a model-free
baseline -- a simple, no-learning scorer (e.g.\ node degree) used as a sanity-check
floor. We audit whether this subfield's evaluation practice inflates reported
performance, across ten cases spanning eight distinct graphs.

\textbf{Methods:} A literature audit of 21 such papers checked split handling,
negative sampling, and model-free-baseline reporting. One of them is ours, built
from a single-cell RNA-seq cohort and miRDB v6.0
interactions; on it we trained a graph transformer and compared a conventional
protocol against one correcting edge leakage and negative-sampling bias. The same heuristics and
architecture, retrained under both protocols, were applied to nine further cases:
two OGB benchmarks and seven survey-paper datasets.

\textbf{Results:} Six of 21 papers report an untrained comparator, clearing it
by 1.7--7.4 points unremarked; 15 report none; 17 use unlabeled-pair negatives. Retraining one architecture under both protocols on six
graphs -- ours and five from surveyed papers -- collapses AUROC from 0.879--0.992
to 0.618--0.641. On our own graph the two
protocols report 0.9867 $\pm$ 0.0011 and 0.6276 $\pm$ 0.0070, the latter within 3.6
points of a no-learning heuristic (0.5912); the inflation recurs across six
architectures and is super-additive, while an untrained control does not move. A
cell-typing task reaches 0.9916 accuracy, surviving an analogous control by 32
points. On two OGB benchmarks our heuristics reproduce the leaderboard exactly. Against the seven papers' own reported numbers,
trained models beat the topology floor by 4.7 points per paper (3.9 per graph), though
the heuristic wins outright on the sparsest.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The inflation is a property of the evaluation protocol, not of
any one architecture or dataset, and this literature does not check for it. We propose a minimum reporting standard: an automated
leak-free split check and a model-free baseline whose margin is stated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Mapping the complex web of biomolecule-biomolecule interactions, particularly microRNA
and drug targets, is essential for advancing modern network biology
\cite{barabasi2004networkbiology,vidal2011interactome}. However,
despite the biological complexity of these relationships,
Graph Neural Network papers predict an AUROC in the
0.91--0.99 range \cite{tian2024mgcnss,jin2022nimgsa,ahmad2026hybridgnn,yu2022hgdti,
cao2024ngcn,zhang2024kmerpmtf,orro2026mirnadisease,
li2025couplemda,zeng2025gonnmda,liu2024digamn,lu2024dgnmda,yu2022hlgnnmda,
huang2022meahne,li2022cksnpgnn,liang2023hmcda,wang2026higldp,xiong2025gpsdti,
zhang2025saegraphdti,yang2025dtimhapr,sun2024ingnndti,jia2024modulepred}. A
literature audit of these 21 papers found that 15 report no model-free baseline --
a simple, no-learning scorer, such as node degree or a common-neighbours count, used
as a sanity-check floor a trained model's performance should be judged against -- that
the six which do report one clear it by between 1.7 and 7.4 AUROC points and none
remarks on how narrow that is, and that 17 of the 21 default to negatives
drawn from unlabeled pairs, without discussion (protocol in Methods, full results in Related Work
below). Neither omission is novel: both are
already documented pitfalls in the broader link-prediction and knowledge-graph
literature, reviewed below. What is missing is the connection between that general
literature and this subfield's own reporting practice -- the general-ML literature
already knows how to check for these pitfalls, but this subfield's papers do not apply
the check.

This paper is structured as an audit of that gap, not a single case report. We measure
what the gap costs by retraining one architecture under both protocols on six graphs --
our own, which we built and can instrument in full, and five taken from the surveyed
papers themselves -- and by comparing nine further externally-sourced cases against a
no-learning floor: two public link-prediction benchmarks and seven of the 21 surveyed
papers' own datasets. Concretely, this paper's contribution is fourfold: it
shows the conventional protocol inflates a trained model by 0.26--0.37 AUROC on every
graph tested, not only on ours; shows the two pitfalls compound rather than simply add;
shows the cost recurs across independently trained architectures rather than being an
artifact of any single model; and shows the same model-free floor is high enough to
matter when measured directly on other papers' own data -- on two of the seven,
decisive -- rather than only inferred by analogy.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Known evaluation pitfalls in link prediction}
Neither failure mode we quantify in this paper is novel to machine learning in general.
Transductive, seen-edge leakage traces at least to the inverse-relation leak documented
in the FB15k knowledge-graph benchmark \cite{toutanova2015observed}, and is actively
being re-examined in the broader link-prediction literature
\cite{zhu2023pitfalls,li2023evaluating}. The same failure recurs closer to this paper's
own domain: a leakage problem inflating reported performance has been independently
identified in miRNA--disease association prediction \cite{dong2020mirnaleakage}, and,
contemporaneously, train/test redundancy has been shown to inflate knowledge-graph-embedding
link prediction across several biomedical graphs -- though that study's own permutation
test found no evidence its models exploit node degree specifically, a useful contrast to
the degree-driven bias we quantify below \cite{briere2026kgeleakage}. Popularity-biased negative sampling is a
known, quantified bias in link prediction generally \cite{kotnis2017analysis,
aiyappa2024implicit}, and has been documented specifically in network biology
\cite{yilmaz2025biasaware}, the domain this paper's graph is drawn from. Its likely
historical root is recommender-systems evaluation practice, where sampling a small set
of negatives per positive was adopted for computational convenience and only later
shown to distort ranking metrics \cite{krichene2020sampled}.

\subsection{How this subfield evaluates itself}
Against that backdrop, we surveyed 21 papers in the miRNA-target and adjacent
biomolecule-interaction link-prediction literature (miRNA-disease, circRNA-disease,
lncRNA-disease, drug-target, and gene-disease), reading each paper's methods section in
full rather than inferring its evaluation protocol from the abstract. 15 of the 21
report no model-free baseline; the six that do -- NIMGSA, NGCN, Orro, HLGNN-MDA,
CKSNP-GNN and ModulePred -- report one of this subfield's classical untrained
propagation or scoring methods (SPM, HNM, TCRWMDA, BNPMDA, LLCMDA, RWR) as one more
competitor in a comparison table, never as a floor: no paper remarks on how narrow its
own margin over that comparator is (\S\ref{sec:generalization} measures those margins,
and what they look like against a floor measured independently). 17 of the 21 draw
their negatives from unlabeled pairs, uniformly sampled or taken wholesale, without
discussing the alternative; only two deviate, MGCNSS and HGDTI, each selecting negatives
by a distance or reliability criterion of its own devising -- an implicit admission that
the default is known to be inadequate -- and the remaining two never say where their
negatives come from. Whether
held-out edges are removed from the encoder's input is unclear from the methods section
in 11 of the 19 papers where the question applies -- the other two encode drug and
protein separately and never propagate over an interaction graph at all, so a held-out
association cannot reach their encoder through graph structure -- up from 3 of the
original 6; this reporting gap, not confirmed leakage,
is now the dominant finding on that dimension. Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}
summarizes the full sample.

We deliberately do not claim that this subfield routinely leaks edges: after
adjudication only 1 of the 19 clearly does -- MGCNSS, whose propagated
matrix is defined globally from all 5,430 known associations -- 7 clearly strip test
edges correctly, and our own project's
original split was worse than the norm we observed. The claim we make is narrower: no
paper in the sample reads an untrained comparator as a floor. Most report none at all,
and the six that do put one on the page treat it as a method to beat rather than as a
sanity check on their own number, none of them commenting on how small the margin it
leaves them is (\S\ref{sec:generalization}). Getting the split right is not protective on its
own -- all seven papers that do strip held-out edges from the graph still draw their
negatives from unlabeled pairs, six uniformly at random and one wholesale, none of them
degree-matched, and four of the seven report no model-free baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}). The two failure modes are
independent: fixing the split is not evidence against the popularity shortcut this
paper identifies, which is precisely why, as Results shows, correcting both is
super-additive rather than redundant.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data and graph construction}
We build a heterogeneous graph from a public single-cell RNA-seq cohort combined with
miRDB v6.0 miRNA--target interactions. The graph has three node types -- miRNA, gene,
and cell -- connected by a miRNA-to-gene regulatory edge, a cell-to-gene expression
edge, and a gene-to-gene co-expression edge, each with an automatically-added reverse
relation for bidirectional message passing (Figure~\ref{fig:graph_schema}). The
disease/cohort label attached to each cell (case vs.\ control) is retained purely as
data provenance: it never enters the graph structure, the model input, the training
objective, or any scoring head, so nothing in this paper's predictions can depend on
it. During preparation of this study we identified and fixed two bugs in the
graph-construction pipeline, in co-expression gene selection and in the cell-to-gene
expression threshold; every headline number reported here was re-measured on the
corrected graph, independently verified, and shown to replicate the pre-fix figures
within one standard deviation.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
We use \emph{transductive evaluation} to name the specific condition audited here: a
held-out edge is left in the message-passing graph when its score is computed, so the
encoder can, in effect, observe the exact edge it is later asked to predict. This is
narrower than the general transductive/inductive distinction in graph learning, which
concerns whether test-time nodes are visible during training and is not by itself a
source of leakage. We split edges with \texttt{RandomLinkSplit} and strip each held-out edge, together
with its automatically-added reverse relation, from the message-passing graph in both
directions, so that no test edge is reachable to the encoder at evaluation time. This
leak-free property is not assumed: it is verified by an automated battery of checks
before any downstream number is trusted (Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split}). The same
hide-and-recover logic is also applied within the training set itself: a fraction of
the training edges are reserved as supervision targets only and withheld from the
encoder's own input, so the model cannot simply read a training edge off the graph
while being asked to predict it. For negative
sampling we compare two regimes, uniform-random and degree-matched, and enforce the
same sampler at training and at evaluation time within any single reported number.
Mixing the two -- training under one regime and evaluating under the other -- produces
what we call the ``mismatch trap'': an apparent difficulty measurement that is in fact
an artifact of the distribution mismatch, which we report as a methodological warning
in its own right (Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}). Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol}
summarizes these two requirements, together with the model-free-baseline requirement
described below, as a minimum reporting checklist. We report AUROC and AUPRC for link
prediction, and accuracy and macro-F1 for cell typing. Headline numbers are averaged
over four independent random seeds where feasible; the six-architecture grid is
measured from a single training run per architecture, trained on one GPU by a driver
separate from the multi-GPU headline runs. Results gives both the effect-size argument
for the single run and the comparability limit the driver difference imposes.

\subsection{Baselines}
For link prediction we report four no-learning topological heuristics -- gene
in-degree, preferential attachment, common neighbours, and Adamic--Adar -- each
computed purely from training-visible edge topology. For cell typing we report two
no-learning or graph-free controls, nearest-centroid and logistic regression, both
trained directly on the raw PCA features without any message passing. Against these
controls we compare six trained architectures: a heterogeneous graph transformer, a
homogeneous GCN, a co-expression-ablation variant of the transformer, a graph-free MLP,
an untrained random-weight control, and a no-miRNA-input ablation that is
link-prediction-incapable by construction and retained only as a smoke test.

\subsection{Literature survey protocol}
The literature survey referenced throughout this paper covers 21 papers, each read in
full at the methods-section level rather than judged from its abstract, and classified
along four dimensions: whether it cross-validates over edges, whether it removes
held-out edges from the encoder's input, how it selects negative examples, and whether
it reports any model-free baseline. This is an expansion, completed 2026-08-12, of an
initial six-paper pilot. The second dimension admits a fourth value, \emph{not
applicable}: two papers encode drug and protein separately -- one as a molecular graph
of atoms and bonds, the other from a contact map or a sequence embedding -- and fuse the
two representations with attention, never propagating over a graph that contains the
associations being predicted. A held-out association cannot reach an encoder of that
shape through graph structure, so the question has no answer to give rather than an
undisclosed one, and those two are excluded from that dimension's denominator instead of
being counted among its \emph{unclear} calls. A twenty-second row of the original survey sheet recorded a
general drug-target field convention rather than a paper, and is excluded from every
denominator reported here.

Every cell was subsequently double-rated. The decision rules, which until then existed
only in the first rater's head and in the evidence quotes, were written up as an
explicit codebook (\texttt{results/SURVEY\_CODEBOOK.md}), and a second pass over all 21
papers was made from the primary sources alone, blind to the first pass. Raw agreement
was 95\% on model-free-baseline reporting, 86\% on the cross-validation unit, 76\% on
negative sampling and 71\% on whether held-out edges are removed from the encoder's
graph; Cohen's $\kappa$ is 0.49 and 0.44 on the latter two, and is uninformative on the
former two, whose marginals are too skewed for chance-correction to behave. All 14 disagreements were adjudicated one at a time
against the primary source rather than averaged away, each call recorded with the
codebook rule that decided it and the verbatim sentence it rests on
(\texttt{results/interrater\_agreement.json},
\texttt{results/literature\_survey\_adjudication.tsv}); 11 went to the second rater and
3 upheld the first. Two of them were settled by a quote the first rater had already
recorded and classified against, and two exposed gaps in the codebook itself rather than
in either reading: it had no rule for a paper that samples negatives one way for training
and another for evaluation, nor for one that describes its negatives for a case study but
not for the benchmark carrying its headline number. Both rules are now written down, and
the affected cells are classified on the arm the reported metric comes from.

Two adjudications changed a headline number. The first was on model-free-baseline
reporting. The raters split on whether ModulePred
reports a model-free baseline, and the disagreement exposed a defect in the original
criterion rather than in either reading: the criterion gave a definition -- a comparator
whose predictions require no parameters fitted to the association data -- and then an
enumerated list of qualifying baselines, and the list omitted this subfield's own
classical untrained methods (random walk with restart, structural perturbation,
path-based and bipartite-projection scoring). The first rater had applied the list, and
recorded \emph{no} for six papers that the definition admits. All 21 papers were
therefore re-audited on that dimension alone under the definition, recording for each
qualifying comparator its name, its reported score, and the surveying paper's own score
on the same metric and dataset, so that the margin between them could be read off
(\texttt{results/literature\_survey\_d4\_reaudit.tsv}). The corrected tallies are the ones
used throughout this paper.

The second was on whether held-out edges are removed from the encoder's input. The first
rater had recorded HGDTI as retaining them, on a paraphrase rather than on a quote; the
codebook requires a \emph{yes} or a \emph{no} to carry a verbatim sentence that supports
it alone, and no such sentence exists in that paper -- it describes its heterogeneous
network and its cross-validation separately and never connects them. The cell became
\emph{unclear}, and the number of surveyed papers that clearly do \emph{not} remove
held-out edges fell from two to one. The surviving one, MGCNSS, states the matrix it
propagates over explicitly, and that matrix is the full set of 5,430 known associations.
An earlier 2026-08-13 re-read of the first rater's own
"unclear" classifications against each paper's primary source, undertaken to check
whether "unclear" reflected a genuine reporting gap or overly conservative extraction,
corrected 3 of 14 spot-checked cells; those corrections are also reflected throughout.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The protocol collapse is a property of the protocol, not of our graph}
\label{sec:collapse_general}
The central measurement of this paper -- what a conventional evaluation protocol costs a
trained model -- can be made on any graph whose association matrix can be obtained, and we
make it on six. Training this paper's architecture from scratch on each of the five
distinct graphs behind the seven surveyed papers, under a protocol we control rather than
each paper's own, reproduces the same collapse every time
(Figure~\ref{fig:cross_graph_collapse}, Table~\ref{tab:discrimination}): AUROC between
0.8789 and 0.9919 under the conventional protocol -- edges visible to the encoder during
message passing, negatives drawn uniformly at random -- falling to between 0.6182 and
0.6406 once test edges are held out and negatives are degree-matched. Six graphs that
share no data, no node types and no size land inside the same 0.023-wide band the moment
the protocol is corrected.

The cost is $+0.256$ to $+0.365$ AUROC. Our own graph's $+0.359$ sits inside that range
but near its upper end: only MEAHNE's $+0.365$ exceeds it. It is a member of this
distribution rather than the outlier a reader might reasonably suspect a single
self-reported graph of being -- though it is not the median member either, and we do not
claim it is. It is also the one graph in the set whose construction and training we
control, so \S\ref{sec:mechanism} uses it to ask why the collapse happens; the effect
itself is the one measured here on all six.

Closing the same grid with the four unmodified topology scorers instead of a trained
model gives the model-free half of the same experiment on every one of these graphs
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol_grid}), and shows the collapse is not something only a trained
model does. Within each row the split and the negatives are held identical across all
four cells and only the adjacency the scorers may see changes, so the cost of the
corrected protocol is attributable to the protocol alone. That cost is $+0.316$ to
$+0.426$ across the six graphs with a full grid, and here our own graph's $+0.342$ sits
inside the range without approaching either edge -- squarely ordinary, where its trained
cost above was ordinary but high. The decomposition, however, differs from ours: on these graphs the negative-sampling axis
carries almost the entire effect ($\approx 0.30$) while the edge-split axis adds only
$0.01$--$0.02$, whereas on our own graph the two axes were comparable and compounded
super-additively. Held-out edges matter less here because these association matrices are
small and their heuristics lean on node degree, which a handful of masked edges barely
moves; the pattern is the same, its internal balance is not.

\input{tables/table6_protocol_grid.tex}

One asymmetry in Table~\ref{tab:discrimination} we can measure but cannot explain. The
\emph{margin} between the trained model and the model-free floor behaves differently on
our graph than on the other five. On all five surveyed graphs the conventional protocol
leaves the two within 1.3 points of each other -- and puts the one-line heuristic ahead
on three of them -- while the corrected protocol opens that margin to between 4.3 and 8.3
points: the conventional protocol cannot tell a trained model from a degree count, and
the corrected one can. On our own graph the margin moves the other way, narrowing from
$+0.053$ to $+0.036$. The obvious candidate explanation is that our graph, unlike the five
bare association matrices, carries real node features and learnable miRNA embeddings, so
the conventional protocol lets the model memorise training edges in a way the corrected
protocol strips -- an advantage the other five graphs have nothing to reproduce. We tested
it and it does not hold: logging training and validation loss per epoch across all six
runs shows the same divergence signature on every graph, including the five with nothing
to memorise. We therefore report the asymmetry as an open observation rather than attach a
mechanism the data does not support, on the same footing as the candidate-space
correlation in \S\ref{sec:dead_columns}. It does not affect the figures themselves: every
checkpoint reported here is selected at peak validation AUROC, before that divergence
begins.

\subsection{Each paper's own reported number against a no-learning floor}
\label{sec:generalization}
The collapse in \S\ref{sec:collapse_general} is measured with our architecture on other
people's graphs. A separate question is how each paper's \emph{own} reported number stands
against a no-learning floor. The literature survey (Section~\ref{sec:related}) raises that
question without settling it: most surveyed papers report no model-free control at all, and
the six that do report one never read it as a floor, so the survey establishes the reporting
practice but not what such a control is worth on these graphs.
Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases} closes that gap across all ten cases examined: our own
miRNA-target graph, two public OGB link-prediction benchmarks (ogbl-ddi, ogbl-ppa)
\cite{hu2020ogb}, and seven of the 21 surveyed papers' own miRNA-disease datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}). Ten cases are not ten independent graphs: three
of the seven surveyed papers -- MGCNSS, NIMGSA and HLGNN-MDA -- evaluate on a
byte-identical HMDD-derived association matrix (495 miRNAs $\times$ 383 diseases,
5,430 associations), so the ten rows span eight distinct graphs. We keep them as
separate rows because each is a separate paper reporting a separate number on that
shared benchmark, and we report the mean gap both per paper ($-0.047$) and per graph
($-0.039$). That a substantial share of this subfield validates on one shared dataset
is itself a fact about its evaluation practice, not a defect of this audit.
Three lines of evidence answer the question, ordered from what this literature has already
published to what we measured ourselves: the six surveyed papers whose own comparison tables
already carry an untrained comparator; an independent re-measurement of that same floor on
their own association matrices; and then the two public benchmarks with open leaderboards and
the seven papers' own data that fill the table in.

\input{tables/table5_hmdd_survey_audit.tex}

\textbf{What the six surveyed papers' own tables already report.} Six of the 21 surveyed
papers do report a comparator that fits nothing: NIMGSA (SPM), NGCN (HNM), Orro (TCRWMDA),
HLGNN-MDA (BNPMDA), CKSNP-GNN (LLCMDA, with PBMDA a second untrained row) and ModulePred
(RWR and RWRH). In the five whose numbers can be read from the text, the paper's own headline
clears its own untrained comparator by 1.7, 1.8, 3.9, 5.0 and 7.4 AUROC points; ModulePred's
appears only in a figure. None of the six remarks on the size of that margin. In every case
the untrained method is presented as one more competitor in a ranking to be won, not as a
floor the headline should be read against -- which is the practice this paper argues is
missing, stated here in the form its own literature has already published. NIMGSA's table
carries the sharpest instance: its SPM comparator (0.8960) comes within 3.9 points of NIMGSA
itself (0.9354) and outscores IMCMDA (0.8329), a trained matrix-completion baseline in the
same table. One caveat belongs with these numbers and is itself a finding about evaluation
practice: CKSNP-GNN did not run its comparators, but reports ``the optimal AUC recorded in
each paper'', so its floor is a number transferred across papers and dataset versions rather
than measured on its own data.

\textbf{The same floor, measured independently.} Three of those six evaluate on an
association matrix we could re-measure, which lets a floor they published be checked against
a floor computed here by a different method (Table~\ref{tab:floor_convergence}). The two
agree. Against untrained comparators those papers report at 0.8960, 0.8565 and 0.9190, our
own best heuristic reaches 0.8842 on the canonical HMDD matrix and 0.8924 on CKSNP-GNN's,
both under the conventional protocol. An untrained scorer reaches 0.86--0.92 on these graphs
however it is built, and that is what makes the papers' own margins interpretable: they are
margins over a floor that replicates across methods and across research groups. Three
qualifications. The trained columns of Table~\ref{tab:floor_convergence} are \emph{not}
comparable -- ours is this paper's architecture, not theirs, under a different fold count and
negative ratio -- so the table is evidence about the floor, not a reproduction of any reported
number. The three papers span two distinct graphs, since NIMGSA and HLGNN-MDA both evaluate
on the canonical 5,430-association matrix. And the floor is a property of the protocol rather
than of the graph alone: under the corrected protocol the same two floors fall to 0.5683 and
0.5591 (Table~\ref{tab:discrimination}).

\textbf{Two public OGB benchmarks.} On ogbl-ddi (drug--drug interaction) and ogbl-ppa
(protein--protein association), both verified leak-free at runtime by the same check
used throughout this paper, the model-free heuristics from Methods reproduce the Open
Graph Benchmark's own published leaderboard numbers exactly (Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases}).
The two benchmarks disagree sharply. On ogbl-ddi, the best heuristic (Adamic--Adar,
Hits@20 0.1861) ranks 30th of 32 leaderboard entries, far behind the best trained
submission (HyperFusion, 0.9972) -- a graph where learning clearly earns its keep. On
ogbl-ppa, Resource Allocation, a zero-parameter formula, ranks 14th of 27 leaderboard
entries (Hits@100 0.4933), essentially tied with the next-best entry, the trained
model BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} (0.4934), and outperforms the eleven trained
or learned methods ranked below it, including canonical graph neural network
architectures such as GCN (0.1867) and GraphSAGE (0.1655), the same architecture
family compared in our own cross-architecture grid. Unlike the papers in our
literature survey, OGB already reports these heuristics alongside trained
submissions -- exactly the practice we recommend -- yet on ogbl-ppa a formula with no
learned parameters still holds its own against a substantial fraction of the trained
methods evaluated against it. Taken together, the two benchmarks show the model-free
floor is not uniformly high or uniformly low across graphs; whether it matters is an
empirical question each graph answers differently, which is precisely why it must be
checked rather than assumed.

\textbf{Seven of the 21 surveyed papers.} We closed the same gap for seven of the 21
surveyed papers (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}), all from its miRNA-disease
cluster, whose data could be independently obtained: their own bundled repositories,
or a shared institutional mirror for one (CKSNP-GNN), each verified against the
paper's own reported node/edge counts before use (three further papers -- GONNMDA,
Orro, DGNMDA -- were excluded because no usable data was recoverable; see Discussion).
Applying the same topology-only scorers, unmodified, to each paper's own association
matrix under uniform-random negatives at that paper's stated ratio
(Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases}), the trained models beat the topology floor by 4.7
AUROC points on average per paper -- a real, externally-measured gap, not merely our
own graph's finding restated. Three of the seven papers (MGCNSS, NIMGSA, HLGNN-MDA)
turn out to evaluate on a byte-identical canonical HMDD matrix, so the seven papers
span five distinct graphs; averaging per graph instead of per paper gives 3.9 points.
But the gap is highly graph-dependent, ranging from $-9.2$
points (DiGAMN) to $+3.3$ points: on MEAHNE's graph, the topology-only heuristic
outright \emph{beats} the paper's own trained model (0.9848 vs.\ 0.9520), and on
CoupleMDA's the two are within 1.3 points. Sanity-checking with degree-matched
negatives collapses every heuristic on every paper to 0.55--0.58, confirming the
uniform-negative numbers reflect a genuine popularity ceiling rather than a data error.

\subsection{What separates these graphs: dead candidate columns}
\label{sec:dead_columns}
The two papers where the trained model adds the least are also the two sparsest graphs
audited (MEAHNE: 17{,}972 edges over 1{,}296 $\times$ 11{,}783 nodes; CoupleMDA:
$\sim$15{,}000 edges over 2{,}090 $\times$ 1{,}754 nodes). Sparsity itself, however, is
not the mechanism. Subsampling positives from each graph in turn (100\% down to 10\%
retention, five seeds per point) moves the model-free floor in the \emph{opposite}
direction: it falls monotonically as the graph is thinned, on all five graphs
(Spearman $\rho = +0.60$ to $+1.00$ against density), and the degree-matched control
falls with it, so thinning simply makes every degree estimate noisier.

What separates these graphs appears instead to be how much of their candidate space can
never be positive. MEAHNE's matrix carries 11{,}783 disease columns of which 92.4\% have
degree zero, and CoupleMDA's 55.7\%, while the other three graphs have none. Across
graphs this is only suggestive and we do not rest the claim on it: with six graphs no
descriptive statistic separates them significantly (dead-column fraction $\rho = +0.70$,
$p = 0.12$; column-degree Gini $\rho = +0.77$, $p = 0.07$), and our own graph is a
counterexample to the simplest version of the story, carrying 47.5\% dead columns yet a
floor indistinguishable from the graphs with none. The evidence is instead a direct
manipulation. Padding the
canonical HMDD matrix -- which has no dead columns -- with empty ones, adding no edges,
no features, nothing but candidate slots that can never be positive, raises its
model-free floor from $0.859 \pm 0.014$ to $0.982 \pm 0.006$; at 92.9\% dead columns,
MEAHNE's own level, it reaches $0.979$, reproducing MEAHNE's outlying $0.9848$ from a
graph sharing none of its data. An AUROC reported under uniform negatives is therefore
partly a measure of how a dataset padded its candidate space, and a padded dataset
hands that inflation to a trained model and to a one-line heuristic alike. We do not
conclude that these five other trained models learned nothing: five of seven still
clearly separate from the topology floor.

Across all nine external cases, the claim is narrower than "the field's models learn
nothing" and already sufficient: \emph{the popularity floor this literature never
checks for is frequently high enough to matter, and on some of its own graphs,
decisive.}

\subsection{Why the collapse happens}
\label{sec:mechanism}
One graph in the set is ours, and it is the one whose construction, features and training
we can vary at will. We use it for the question the other five cannot answer: not whether
the collapse happens -- \S\ref{sec:collapse_general} settles that on all six -- but what
produces it.

The two corrections are not redundant. Crossing them independently
(Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid}, Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid}) leaves a substantially
inflated number when either is applied alone, and only correcting both reaches 0.6276
$\pm$ 0.0070: the pair is super-additive (the exact per-term attribution is
convention-sensitive, as Methods discusses, so we report the total rather than a
decomposition). Neither figure is an artifact of one snapshot of the graph -- an
independently applied fix to two graph-construction bugs reproduces both within 0.1
standard deviations, and leaves the topology-only floor numerically identical, since it
touched only co-expression and cell-expression edges rather than the miRNA-to-gene
topology the floor is computed from.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Each half of the inflation we measure already has a name in the broader link-prediction
literature, and neither is specific to microRNAs. The edge-split half is an instance of
what \cite{zhu2023pitfalls} call \emph{target-link inclusion} -- the target edge remains
reachable to the encoder through message passing at evaluation time, so the model can, in
effect, recover the label from the neighborhood it was never supposed to have. Its
lineage traces to the WN18/FB15k inverse-relation leak documented by
\cite{toutanova2015observed}, which was serious enough to motivate a replacement
benchmark (FB15k-237) rather than a footnote; Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split} is the same
mechanism restated for a bipartite miRNA--gene graph with explicit reverse relations
in place of duplicated inverse triples. The negative-sampling half is \emph{degree bias}
(also \emph{popularity bias}): \cite{aiyappa2024implicit} name and quantify it generally,
and \cite{yilmaz2025biasaware} document the identical pattern in network biology
specifically, the domain this graph is drawn from. Mechanistically, degree bias is a
textbook case of \emph{shortcut learning} \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}: a model trained
under uniform negatives has no incentive to learn miRNA-specific regulatory signal when
gene in-degree alone -- a feature that does not even require knowing which miRNA is
being scored -- separates positives from negatives almost for free
(Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}); collapsing to chance the moment that shortcut
is closed off (Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}) is the signature of a model that
learned the shortcut and nothing else, not of a harder evaluation. The historical root of
treating unlabeled pairs as negatives at all traces to sampled-metric evaluation practice
in recommender systems \cite{krichene2020sampled}, a convention that migrated into link
prediction without a matching re-derivation of when it is and is not valid.

What we have not found named in the literature is the interaction between the two: every
prior treatment we are aware of studies target-link inclusion or degree bias in
isolation. Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid} and Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} show they are
super-additive on this graph -- correcting either alone leaves the number substantially
inflated, and the cross-architecture grid (Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid},
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_inflation}) shows the same compounding recurs across six
independently-trained architectures, including a graph-free MLP, with the untrained
control the only one that does not move. We propose calling this \emph{compounded
evaluation inflation} to distinguish it from either pitfall reported alone: the
practical implication is that auditing a paper for target-link inclusion \emph{or} for a
model-free baseline, but not both, can still miss a substantially inflated headline
number.

Neither result above is confined to our own graph. The compounded inflation itself
reproduces on every graph we could retrain on: five externally-sourced association
matrices, sharing no data with ours and none of its node features, each lose 0.256 to
0.365 AUROC between the two protocols and each land in the same 0.618--0.641 band
(\S\ref{sec:collapse_general}, Table~\ref{tab:discrimination}). That is the strongest
form the claim takes -- the inflation is a property of the protocol, measurable wherever
the protocol is applied -- and it is what licenses the title's framing over the weaker
reading that we found a quirk of one graph. The separate weakness of trained models
relative to a model-free floor also recurs, more unevenly, across the nine further
cases (seven distinct graphs) that make up this paper's validation set
(Section~\ref{sec:generalization}, Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases}). That weakness is
not only ours to measure: six of the surveyed papers put an untrained comparator in their
own comparison tables and clear it by 1.7 to 7.4 AUROC points without comment, over a floor
that replicates when the same matrices are scored here by a different method
(Table~\ref{tab:floor_convergence}). Topological heuristics have also been shown
competitive with, or superior to, trained neural link predictors elsewhere in the
literature, corroborating the pattern from an independent direction: on realistic
biomedical graphs, a plain Common Neighbours baseline holds its own against
graph-embedding neural methods on small or sparse, highly-disconnected graphs
\cite{crichton2018realistic}, and a purely topology-centric method has been shown to
outperform state-of-the-art GNN link predictors in accuracy while requiring a fraction
of the training and inference cost \cite{huang2023gelato}. Notably, on ogbl-ppa this
holds even though OGB already reports model-free heuristics alongside trained
submissions -- exactly the practice we recommend -- which suggests the gap is not only
that model-free baselines go unreported, but that reporting one does not, by itself,
raise the bar the field treats as meaningful progress.

Seven limitations qualify these results. First, the cross-architecture grid reports a
single measurement per architecture rather than an average over multiple training
runs; we consider this justified because the inflation effect size is far larger than
the run-to-run variability established elsewhere in this study, but a fully repeated
version of the grid would still strengthen the claim. Second, the task's information
ceiling is bounded by what the graph and its labels can support: the graph itself
carries no miRNA or gene sequence information, and the interaction label source, miRDB,
is itself the output of a seed-complementarity prediction model rather than an
experimentally confirmed interaction. This ceiling would not be resolved by a better
architecture; it would require predicting \emph{experimentally validated} interactions
-- for example, from miRTarBase -- instead of another predictor's output. Third, the
model-free baselines reported here are local neighborhood-overlap heuristics -- gene
degree, preferential attachment, common neighbours, and Adamic--Adar -- computed only
from the miRNA-to-gene adjacency; we did not evaluate global, path-integrating
heuristics such as the Katz index or rooted PageRank, which are known to outperform
local heuristics on sparse graphs and which the trained model's multi-hop,
multi-relation message passing more closely resembles. The 3.6-point gap between the
best local heuristic and the trained model should therefore be read as an upper bound
on how much the model exceeds a no-learning topological floor, not a tight one. Fourth,
the six trained architectures compared here (Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid}) are all
either generic message-passing networks (the project HGT, a homogeneous GCN) or a
graph-free MLP; none explicitly encodes pairwise structural signal -- shared neighbors,
enclosing-subgraph structure, or path distance -- the way link-prediction-specific
architectures such as SEAL \cite{zhang2018seal}, NBFNet \cite{zhu2021nbfnet}, Neo-GNN
\cite{yun2021neognn}, or BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} do. Architectures of that
family place above local heuristics on properly-audited external leaderboards,
including the OGB benchmarks discussed above, where BUDDY narrowly outperforms
Resource Allocation on ogbl-ppa. The 3.6-point gap measured here should therefore not be read as a ceiling on
what any trained model could achieve on this graph under the corrected protocol -- only
on what the generic message-passing architectures tested here achieve; whether a
structure-aware architecture closes it is an open question this study does not
answer. Fifth, the 21-paper literature survey (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}) is a
two-rater instrument with imperfect agreement, not a settled measurement. Both raters
read the primary sources under a written codebook, and every disagreement was
adjudicated against the source rather than averaged away, but agreement before
adjudication was moderate on the two dimensions that carry real spread: Cohen's
$\kappa$ 0.44 on whether held-out edges are removed from the encoder's input and 0.49
on negative sampling. The "unclear" calls in particular -- 11 of 19 on the former --
should be read as an adjudicated two-rater judgment rather than a fact a third reader
would recover with certainty. We report the double-rating as a limitation and not as a
resolution because it changed a headline: the first rater's classification, from which
this paper's earlier drafts concluded that no surveyed paper reports a model-free
baseline at all, was overturned to six of 21 once the raters' one disagreement on that
dimension exposed a defect in the criterion itself (Methods). A criterion defect of
that kind is precisely what a second rater is for, and we cannot rule out another one
in the dimensions where agreement is only moderate. What the correction did not do is
weaken the survey's argument: it replaced a claim about an absence with a claim about a
margin, which the six papers' own tables state and none of them discusses. Sixth, the seven-paper topology audit
(Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases}) reconstructs, rather than reproduces, six of seven
papers' evaluation protocols: only MGCNSS's split and CoupleMDA's positive edges are
each paper's own exact data, the rest use an exact association matrix (verified against
each paper's own reported node/edge counts) under a self-generated split at the paper's
stated negative ratio, since no further split file was published. Three further
candidate papers from the same survey cluster (GONNMDA, Orro, DGNMDA) were excluded
from the audit entirely rather than approximated further: GONNMDA's linked repository
contains no code or data, Orro's data-availability statement provides no public
repository, and DGNMDA's cited data source was unreachable at the time of this audit.
This audit was further scoped, by design, to the miRNA-disease cluster of the 21-paper
survey -- 10 of its 11 miRNA-disease papers, of which 7 yielded usable data; the
eleventh (HybridGNN) was not carried into the audit. The other 10 surveyed papers span
drug-target (6 papers), circRNA-disease, lncRNA-disease, gene-disease, and plant
miRNA-target interactions, each keyed to a different reference
database (e.g.\ DrugBank, BindingDB) that this audit did not attempt to acquire;
extending the same protocol to those domains is future work, not a gap left
unexplained. The floor-convergence check in Table~\ref{tab:floor_convergence} is narrower
still: three papers over two distinct graphs, one of which (CKSNP-GNN) reports a comparator
number transferred from that comparator's own paper rather than measured on its data, so the
agreement between the published and the re-measured floor rests on two independent matrices,
not three. The audit's headline number -- a 4.7-point average gap per paper, 3.9 per
distinct graph, reversing sign on one of the seven -- should be read as a
lower-confidence, but externally-sourced, complement to the single-graph result in
Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}, not a literal replication of any individual
paper's reported figure, and not yet evidence about the 12 domains it does not cover.

Seventh, the six-graph retraining experiment (Table~\ref{tab:discrimination},
Figure~\ref{fig:cross_graph_collapse}) trains \emph{our} architecture on each paper's
graph, not each paper's own model, which we could not in general reproduce. It therefore
licenses the claim that the conventional protocol inflates \emph{a} trained model on
those graphs -- the claim this paper makes -- and not the stronger claim that the
specific figures those papers report are inflated by that amount, which would require
re-running their models under both protocols. The two arms are kept in separate tables
for this reason: Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases} reports each paper's own number
against a floor, Table~\ref{tab:discrimination} reports ours under both protocols, and
the two should not be read across. A related open item sits inside the same experiment:
the trained-versus-floor margin widens under the corrected protocol on all five external
graphs but narrows on ours, and the one mechanism we tested for it -- memorisation
afforded by our graph's node features, absent from the five bare association matrices --
is ruled out by per-epoch loss curves that show the same divergence on all six. We
report it as an open observation (\S\ref{sec:collapse_general}) rather than resolve it.

A related check follows from the same logic and generalizes beyond this study. Any claim
that an interaction is predicted in a context-specific way -- cell-type-specific
regulation, say -- should be tested against three questions before it is trusted: whether
the context variable is present in the training data at all, whether the scoring head
receives it as an input, and whether the reported evaluation actually varies with it. The
scoring head used here fails the second by construction, taking only the concatenated
miRNA and gene embeddings, so its score for a given pair is identical in every cell type
however long it is trained; we therefore attach no context-specific narrative to these
results. The check costs nothing to run and, like a model-free baseline, is most valuable
exactly where it is least likely to be run.

Given how directly the inflation problem responds to a small set of checks, we propose
these as a minimum reporting standard for evaluation methodology in this subfield: an
automated, leak-free split check that verifies held-out edges are unreachable to the
encoder during message passing; at least one model-free baseline reported alongside any
trained model, \emph{and the margin over it stated} -- six of the surveyed papers
report such a comparator and none reads its own margin; and negative-sampling distributions that are matched between training
and evaluation rather than assumed compatible (summarized as a checklist in
Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Evaluating a trained link predictor under conventions common in this subfield -- edges
visible to the encoder during message passing, negatives sampled uniformly at random --
reports AUROC between 0.879 and 0.992 on six biomedical interaction graphs; correcting
both conventions simultaneously drops every one of them into the range 0.618--0.641.
Five of those graphs are other groups' published association matrices, sharing no data
with ours, so the inflation is not a property of any one dataset. On the graph we built
and can instrument in full, the same two corrections take 0.9867 to 0.6276, within 3.6
points of a one-line, no-learning topological heuristic, and they are super-additive, so
auditing for either pitfall alone is not sufficient to catch the inflation. The same
compounding recurs across six independently trained architectures, including a
graph-free MLP, while an untrained control does not move, and a structurally similar
but genuinely unaffected cell-typing control task survives the identical audit by a
wide margin -- together showing that the inflation is a property of the evaluation
protocol itself, not of any one architecture, and that the audit is discriminating
rather than indiscriminate. Beyond this specific graph, we contribute a reusable
methodology: an automated leak-free split check, model-free baselines for both link
prediction and node classification, and a cross-architecture protocol grid, released
alongside this paper so that other groups working on biomedical interaction graphs can
run the same audit on their own models before reporting a headline number.
