\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Objective:} We test whether conventional evaluation of graph neural network (GNN) link predictors on biomedical interaction graphs inflates reported performance, where the inflation comes from, and whether a reporting standard and a tool can prevent it.

\textbf{Methods:} We compared a conventional protocol (edges seen during message passing, uniform negatives) with a corrected one (held-out edges, degree-matched negatives). We retrained one architecture on six graphs (ours and five from published papers) and scored four model-free heuristics (parameter-free topology scores) on ten cases: those graphs, two Open Graph Benchmark (OGB) datasets and seven papers from a 21-paper survey. We also added empty candidate columns to a matrix that had none, compared four trained models and a cell-typing task on our graph, and validated the tool, protocheck, on 80 audits with known ground truth.

\textbf{Results:} The conventional protocol overstated the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC): 0.879--0.992 fell to 0.612--0.633 on every graph. Negative sampling carried 0.30--0.45 of the heuristics' loss on the five published graphs, a sixth or more of it label noise on the one graph we could check; on ours the two failures compound. Empty candidate columns raised a model-free floor from 0.859 to 0.982. Across seven papers, trained models beat that floor by 4.7 points, and the heuristic won on the sparsest graph; rerun with their own code, six papers' models fell from 0.86--1.00 to 0.50--0.75, at most 2.8 points above the floor. Four trained models inflated by 0.24--0.31, a graph-free MLP included; cell typing kept a 32-point margin. \texttt{protocheck} flagged every seeded leak and negative mismatch, without false alarms.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The inflation follows the evaluation protocol, not the model or dataset. We propose a minimum reporting standard (a leak-free edge split, matched negative sampling, and a model-free baseline with its margin stated); \texttt{protocheck} verifies it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Mapping the complex web of biomolecule-biomolecule interactions, particularly microRNA
and drug targets, is essential for advancing modern network biology
\cite{vidal2011interactome}. However,
despite the biological complexity of these relationships, Graph Neural Network papers
in this space report high performance \cite{tian2024mgcnss,jin2022nimgsa,
ahmad2026hybridgnn,yu2022hgdti,cao2024ngcn,zhang2024kmerpmtf,orro2026mirnadisease,
li2025couplemda,zeng2025gonnmda,liu2024digamn,lu2024dgnmda,yu2022hlgnnmda,
huang2022meahne,li2022cksnpgnn,liang2023hmcda,wang2026higldp,xiong2025gpsdti,
zhang2025saegraphdti,yang2025dtimhapr,sun2024ingnndti,jia2024modulepred}. A literature
audit of 21 papers in this space found three gaps behind those numbers. First, 15 report no
model-free baseline (a simple scorer with no trained parameters, such as node degree, used as a
sanity-check floor). Second, 16 draw their negatives from unlabeled pairs without
discussion. Third, for 12 of the 19 papers where the question applies (the other two never
propagate over an interaction graph), it is unclear whether held-out edges are removed from
the encoder's input (full results in Related Work below). The last two
gaps correspond to two protocol failure modes: popularity-biased negative sampling and
transductive edge leakage. The first gap is a reporting omission. It is the check that
would expose the other two. Neither failure mode is novel. Both are already documented
in the broader link-prediction and knowledge-graph literature, reviewed below. What is
missing is the connection between that general literature and this subfield's own
reporting practice. The general-ML literature already knows how to check for these
failure modes, but this subfield's papers do not apply the check.

This paper asks whether these high numbers are inflated by the evaluation protocol, rather
than reflecting any one model or dataset. The survey of 21 papers shows what they leave
unreported. We then measure what that costs. We retrain one architecture under both
protocols on six graphs (our own, which we built, and five taken from the surveyed
papers), separate the two failure modes on each with model-free heuristics, and compare nine
further cases against a model-free floor: two public link-prediction benchmarks, and seven
of the surveyed papers, evaluated on their own datasets. Our own graph is one of the six.
Because we built it, it is also the one on which we can dissect the mechanism in full.
This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It quantifies the inflation on six graphs. The conventional protocol overstates AUROC by
0.26--0.38 on every one of them, and the corrected protocol leaves all six at 0.61--0.63.
\item It explains where the inflation comes from. It separates the two failure modes, whose
relative weight depends on the graph, and shows that candidate columns which can never be
positive raise the model-free floor on their own.
\item It measures the model-free floor on other papers' own data: seven surveyed papers and
two Open Graph Benchmark (OGB) datasets \cite{hu2020ogb}.
\item On a proposed graph, it shows the effect follows the
protocol and not the model. It recurs across four trained models, a graph-free MLP
included, and a cell-typing task survives the same audit.
\item It proposes a minimum reporting standard with three requirements (a leak-free edge
split, matched negative sampling, and a model-free baseline with its margin stated;
Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol}) and releases \texttt{protocheck}, an open-source tool
that runs these checks on a user's own train and held-out pairs, so that anyone can check
whether their data are ready before reporting a number.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Known evaluation pitfalls in link prediction}
Neither failure mode we quantify in this paper is novel to machine learning in general.
Transductive edge leakage, where a held-out edge stays in the graph the encoder propagates over (Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split}), traces at least to the inverse-relation leak documented
in FB15k, a knowledge-graph benchmark derived from Freebase \cite{toutanova2015observed}, and is actively
being re-examined in the broader link-prediction literature
\cite{zhu2023pitfalls,li2023evaluating}. The same failure recurs closer to this paper's
own domain: a leakage problem inflating reported performance has been independently
identified in miRNA--disease association prediction \cite{dong2020mirnaleakage}, and,
contemporaneously, train/test redundancy has been shown to inflate knowledge-graph-embedding
link prediction across several biomedical graphs, although that study's own permutation
test found no evidence its models exploit node degree specifically, a useful contrast to
the degree-driven bias we quantify below \cite{briere2026kgeleakage}.

Popularity-biased negative sampling (Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}) is a known, quantified bias in link prediction generally \cite{kotnis2017analysis,
aiyappa2024implicit}, and has been documented specifically in network biology
\cite{yilmaz2025biasaware}, the domain this paper's graph is drawn from. Its likely
historical root is recommender-systems evaluation practice, where sampling a small set
of negatives per positive was adopted for computational convenience and only later
shown to distort ranking metrics \cite{krichene2020sampled}. Table~\ref{tab:known_pitfalls}
summarizes the precedents. The third element of the reporting standard, the model-free baseline, is a reporting omission rather than a failure mode. It has precedent too, and its prevalence in this subfield is measured in the survey below.

\subsection{Evaluation practice in the literature}
Against that backdrop, we surveyed 21 papers in the miRNA-target and adjacent
biomolecule-interaction link-prediction literature (miRNA-disease, circRNA-disease,
lncRNA-disease, drug-target, and gene-disease), reading each paper's methods section in
full rather than inferring its protocol from the abstract
(Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}). Three findings matter here. First, 15 of the 21
report no model-free baseline. The six that do report a classical untrained method as one
more competitor in a comparison table, never as a floor, and none states its margin over
that comparator. In the five that print the numbers in the text or a table, the headline
clears the comparator by 1.7 to 7.4 AUROC points; NIMGSA's \cite{jin2022nimgsa} SPM
comparator (0.8960) comes within 3.9 points of NIMGSA itself (0.9354) and outscores IMCMDA

expression, and cell nodes a 50-dimensional PCA projection of their expression profile. The disease label attached to each cell is kept

\paragraph{Candidate columns} For each graph we report two properties of its candidate columns, the diseases or genes a row can be paired with. A column is \emph{empty} when no association is known for it, and \emph{Empty} is the fraction of such columns. \emph{Gini} is the Gini coefficient of the column degrees, taken over all columns including the empty ones: 0 when every column is equally connected, 1 when all edges fall on one column.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Link prediction scores node pairs $(u,v)$. Let $E$ be the set of target edges, the links
to predict: miRNA$\to$gene edges in our graph, miRNA--disease associations in the surveyed
matrices, and the benchmark's own edges in OGB. Any other edge types a graph has (in ours,
cell$\to$gene and gene--gene) are never held out. We evaluate every model under two
protocols. The \emph{conventional} protocol holds nothing out: every edge of $E$ stays in
the message-passing graph and is also a supervision and evaluation target (``edges
seen''), and negatives are drawn uniformly at random. The \emph{corrected} protocol holds test edges out of the message-passing graph and
draws degree-matched negatives. The data, labels, model and metric are the same in both. Crossing the two choices gives a $2\times2$ grid of edge
split by negative sampler (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} gives the four cells for our model), which lets us attribute the inflation to each failure
separately (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). Table~\ref{tab:protocol_arms} gives the
difference between the two arms.

\input{tables/table_protocol_arms.tex}

We use \emph{transductive edge leakage} for the specific condition audited here: a
held-out edge left in the message-passing graph when its score is computed, so the
encoder can, in effect, observe the edge it is later asked to predict, narrower than
the general transductive/inductive distinction in graph learning, which is not itself a
source of leakage. In the corrected protocol, 10\% of the edges of $E$ are held
out for validation and 10\% for testing at random, and each held-out edge, with its
automatically-added reverse relation, is removed from the message-passing graph in both
directions. An automated battery of checks verifies this before any downstream number is
trusted (Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split}). The same logic applies within the training set: 30\%
of the training edges are withheld from the encoder's input and used only as supervision
targets, so the model cannot read a training edge off the graph while being asked to
predict it.

\phantomsection\label{sec:methods_negatives}%
A uniform negative pairs the source node $u$ of a positive with a target node drawn
uniformly at random. A degree-matched negative pairs it instead with a non-target node of
similar popularity: target nodes are placed in eight log-spaced bins of in-degree,
computed from training edges only, and each positive $(u,v)$ receives a negative $(u,v')$
with $v'$ in the same bin as $v$:
\begin{equation}
v' \sim \mathcal{U}\bigl\{\, j : b(j) = b(v),\ (u,j)\notin E \,\bigr\},
\qquad b(j)=\mathrm{bin}_8\bigl(\log(1+d_{\mathrm{tr}}(j))\bigr),
\label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{tr}}(j)$ is the in-degree of node $j$ among training edges.
During training, negatives are drawn per batch with up to 16 draws from that set and a uniform
non-target of $u$ as the fallback. Evaluation negatives are drawn from the set itself, enumerated
rather than sampled; when it is empty, because $u$ already targets every node in the bin, they come
from the nearest bin that is not. On our graph the bins never run out, but on the five external
graphs 3--4\% of held-out positives hit an exhausted bin, and a uniform fallback for those few
raises the model-free floor by 0.016--0.046 AUROC (\texttt{analysis/test\_fallback\_policy.py}).
Within any single reported number the same type of negative, uniform or degree-matched, is used
at training and at evaluation;

trap'', an apparent difficulty measurement that is in fact an artifact of the distribution
mismatch (Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}). In the headline grid
(Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid}), the edges-seen row reports the uniform-negative-trained model
under both evaluation regimes, and the edges-held-out row each model scored with the negative
type it was trained on. The third requirement of the standard we
propose concerns reporting rather than the protocol itself: a model-free baseline, whose
margin over the trained model is stated alongside the headline number
(\S\ref{sec:baselines}).

We report AUROC for link prediction, and accuracy and macro-F1 for cell
typing, averaged over four random seeds where feasible. The model grid is a
single run per model, on one GPU, by a driver separate from the multi-GPU
headline runs; Results gives the effect-size argument for the single run and the
comparability limit the driver difference imposes.

\subsection{Baselines and models}
\label{sec:baselines}
\paragraph{Model-free baselines} A model-free baseline is a comparator that does not use
the machinery of the model under test. Its score is the floor a trained model must clear,
and the margin is the model's score minus that floor. In link prediction it is a
parameter-free topological heuristic: it ranks candidate pairs from the training-visible
structure of the graph alone, so it measures how much of a reported AUROC the topology
already provides. We use four (Table~\ref{tab:heuristics}), computed on the training edges
only, and call them the model-free heuristics; the model-free floor on a graph is the AUROC
of the best of them. Gene in-degree doubles as a sanity check: against degree-matched
negatives it should sit near 0.5 by construction. On the external matrices the floor is an
AUROC, and on OGB it is Hits@$K$, the metric of the public leaderboards. For cell typing
the equivalent comparator is a graph-free classifier on the same input features: nearest
centroid, which has no fitted parameters beyond the class means, and logistic regression, a
fitted linear model, both trained directly on the raw PCA features without message passing.

\input{tables/table_heuristics.tex}

On the two OGB graphs, which are homogeneous and undirected, we use the textbook one-hop forms of the same scores on the symmetrized training edges, plus Resource Allocation \cite{zhou2009local}, the strongest heuristic on ogbl-ppa's leaderboard; negatives are OGB's official ones.

\paragraph{Models} The link-prediction experiments compare these baselines with four
trained models (a heterogeneous graph transformer, a homogeneous GCN, a
co-expression-ablation variant of the transformer, and a graph-free MLP) plus a
random-score reference that ignores its input and marks chance level.

\subsection{Inflation across six graphs (E1, E2)}

not affect the figures, since every checkpoint is selected at peak validation AUROC, before

\subsection{Published results against a model-free floor (E3, E4)}
\label{sec:generalization}
Table~\ref{tab:validation_cases} gives the ten cases, and four results stand out. First, on
the seven surveyed papers' own matrices the trained models beat the model-free floor by 4.7
AUROC points on average per paper (3.9 per distinct graph), but the gap runs from $-9.2$
points (DiGAMN~\cite{liu2024digamn}) to $+3.3$: on MEAHNE's~\cite{huang2022meahne} graph the heuristic beats the paper's own model
(0.9848 vs.\ 0.9520), and with degree-matched negatives every heuristic falls to
0.52--0.55. Second, the floor replicates. The untrained comparators that NIMGSA~\cite{jin2022nimgsa}, HLGNN-MDA~\cite{yu2022hlgnnmda}
and CKSNP-GNN~\cite{li2022cksnpgnn} report (0.8960, 0.8565 and 0.9190) sit beside the 0.8842 and 0.8924 that our
best heuristic reaches on their matrices under the conventional protocol, and CKSNP-GNN's
is a number transferred from other papers, not measured on its own data. Third, on OGB the
heuristics reproduce the leaderboards exactly and the floor is not uniform: on ogbl-ddi the
best heuristic ranks 30th of 32~\cite{ogb2026leaderboard}, while on ogbl-ppa a zero-parameter formula ranks 14th of 27,
essentially tied with the entry just above it, BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} (0.4933 vs.\
0.4934 Hits@100).

Fourth, for six papers we could measure the inflation of their own models rather than ours, because all six
released their code, and in both the code shows what the text does not (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}).
NIMGSA's~\cite{jin2022nimgsa} folds remove held-out miRNAs' rows from the model's input, but the reported
AUROC is computed over the whole matrix, training rows included, over a miRNA similarity graph that is fixed
across folds and 73.6\% a Gaussian interaction-profile kernel of the full association matrix. Its released
code reproduces the paper's 0.9354 exactly (first fold, published seed; 0.9366 over five seeds) and,
corrected one step at a time (Table~\ref{tab:nimgsa_rerun}), falls to 0.921 when only held-out rows are
scored, to 0.856 when the kernel is recomputed without them, and to 0.526 with degree-matched negatives,
0.1 points above a disease's training degree. HybridGNN's~\cite{ahmad2026hybridgnn} text describes an
80/10/10 edge split with a validation set, but its code builds the message-passing graph, the similarity
features and a matrix-factorisation feature from the full association matrix, test edges included, and
keeps the epoch with the best test AUROC. With the paper's hyper-parameters it reproduces the 0.9765 of
the paper's ablation table and repository (0.976; Table~\ref{tab:hybridgnn_rerun}; its abstract gives 0.9715). Selecting the epoch on a
validation split costs almost nothing, building the graph and features from training edges costs 4.6
points (0.930, 0.8 above the floor), and degree-matched negatives bring it to 0.518, below the 0.523 that
disease degree reaches; on the canonical HMDD matrix it ends 2.8 points under the floor without leakage
and at the floor with matched negatives. HGDTI~\cite{yu2022hgdti}, a drug--target model whose module is released
only compiled (we rebuilt it from its bytecode), scores 0.9995 with its own pipeline, which propagates over an
adjacency built from the test labels and samples similarity-filtered ``reliable'' negatives before the split.
Propagating over training edges, then uniform and degree-matched test negatives give 0.946, 0.880 and 0.573
(Table~\ref{tab:hgdti_rerun}); the last is 17 points under a three-path heuristic (0.740) that tracks target
families, and the same model trained on random negatives scores higher on both corrected tests.
kmerPMTF~\cite{zhang2024kmerpmtf}, a plant miRNA--target model, removes each fold's test pairs from its training
graph but is evaluated over a second graph, every edge except the training pairs, so the held-out positives are
edges of the graph its spectral layer propagates over at test time. With the configuration its README gives,
its code scores 0.855--0.909 on the paper's four species (the paper reports 0.80--0.91;
Table~\ref{tab:kmerpmtf_rerun}); evaluated over the training graph, the same models score 0.40--0.64, under a
common-neighbour or Adamic--Adar floor on every species, and 0.50--0.52 with degree-matched negatives. Against
every remaining unlabeled pair, the negatives its text describes, they clear the floor by 5 to 20 points, so
there is signal beyond popularity on that easier test, but not on the 1:1 negatives its code draws. Under these
four published evaluations, handing the input back unchanged scores more than the model (0.970 for NIMGSA, 1.000
for HybridGNN, HGDTI and kmerPMTF). NGCN~\cite{cao2024ngcn}, on the same drug--target data as HGDTI, does not leak: its model
never sees a drug--target edge, and features built without any (DTINet's~\cite{luo2017dtinet}) score the same.
It reproduces 0.924 (the paper reports 0.910; Table~\ref{tab:ngcn_rerun}), but 74\% of its uniform negatives sit
on proteins with no interaction, so protein degree alone reaches 0.886. Degree-matched negatives alone, with no
leak to remove, cost it 17 to 25 points: trained on as many negatives as positives it ends 5.3 points under the
three-path floor (0.671 against 0.725), and trained on ten times as many it clears it by 2.8 (0.753).
CoupleMDA~\cite{li2025couplemda} holds out a fixed test set that its graph never sees (42 of its 1,523 test
positives, 2.8\%, are nonetheless also training edges), but its negatives, for training and test alike, keep

\subsection{Why the floor is high on some graphs: empty columns (E5)}
\label{sec:dead_columns}
In a miRNA--disease matrix the columns are the diseases a miRNA can be paired with, and a
column is \emph{empty} when no association is known for it, so it can never be a positive.
Under uniform negatives such columns supply negatives that any scorer aware of degree
rejects for free. MEAHNE~\cite{huang2022meahne} has 11{,}783 disease columns, 92.4\% of them empty, and CoupleMDA~\cite{li2025couplemda}
55.7\%, while the other three external graphs have none (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_grid}).

\input{tables/table6_protocol_grid.tex}

Sparsity is not the explanation: thinning the positives of each graph lowers the
model-free floor on all five external graphs (Spearman $\rho = +0.60$ to $+1.00$ against
density) instead of raising it. Across the six graphs no descriptive statistic separates
them significantly (empty-column fraction $\rho = +0.70$, $p = 0.12$; column-degree Gini
$\rho = +0.77$, $p = 0.07$), and our own graph is a counterexample, with 47.5\% empty columns
and a floor indistinguishable from the graphs with none. The direct evidence is a
manipulation. Adding empty columns to the canonical HMDD~\cite{li2014hmdd} matrix, which has none, and nothing
else, raises its model-free floor from $0.859 \pm 0.014$ to $0.982 \pm 0.006$. At 92.9\%
empty columns, close to MEAHNE's 92.4\%, it reaches $0.979$, reproducing MEAHNE's outlying $0.9848$
from a graph that shares none of its data.

\subsection{Sources of the inflation on our graph (E6)}
\label{sec:mechanism}
The two corrections are not redundant. From the conventional 0.9867, matched negatives alone

is super-additive. How the total divides depends on which is applied first (the negatives
lower it by 0.062 with edges seen but 0.182 with edges held out), so we report the total
rather than a decomposition.

\subsection{Cell-type classification control (E7)}
\label{sec:celltype}
The audit does not deflate every number it touches. This control moves from link prediction to a
different task on the same graph: classifying the cell type of each cell (node classification). The
label of a cell is the highest marker-gene score computed from its expression, and the only cell
feature the model sees is a PCA of that same expression, so the 0.9916 test accuracy could
be the model recomputing its own label. Two graph-free controls on the PCA features alone, with no
graph and no message passing, test this: nearest-centroid reaches 0.4654 and logistic regression
0.6692 (Table~\ref{tab:celltype_control}). The trained model's gap to the better control, 32 points,
is an order of magnitude larger than the 3.6 points the corrected link-prediction protocol leaves
between the trained model and its topological floor. Here the graph and message passing do
substantial work, and the same methodology leaves the number intact: the audit is
discriminating rather than indiscriminate.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Collapsing to chance the moment gene in-degree is closed off (Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}) is the
signature of shortcut learning \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}, not of a harder evaluation. What we have not found
named in the literature is the interaction between the two failures: prior work studies transductive edge leakage
or popularity-biased negative sampling in isolation (Section~\ref{sec:related}, Table~\ref{tab:known_pitfalls}),
but on our graph they are super-additive (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid}), so auditing for one and not the other
can still miss an inflated headline. We call this \emph{compounded evaluation inflation}. Empty candidate
columns act through the second failure: under uniform negatives they supply negatives that any degree-aware
scorer rejects for free, raising the model-free floor from 0.859 to 0.982 with no learning
(Section~\ref{sec:dead_columns}). The model-free baseline is not a third source of inflation; it is what makes
the other two visible.

None of this is specific to our graph or to one model. Five external association matrices that share no data
with ours each lose 0.26--0.38 AUROC between the protocols and land in the same 0.612--0.633 band
(Table~\ref{tab:discrimination}); four trained models inflate by 0.24--0.31, a graph-free MLP
included, and cell typing on the same graph survives the same audit by 32 points (Section~\ref{sec:celltype}). The
weakness against a floor also appears in published work. Across the seven surveyed papers the trained models
beat the floor by 4.7 points on average, from 9.2 points above it (DiGAMN~\cite{liu2024digamn}) to 3.3 below it (MEAHNE~\cite{huang2022meahne}), and on
ogbl-ppa a zero-parameter formula ranks 14th of 27, tied with BUDDY~\cite{chamberlain2023buddy}. OGB's leaderboards already list such heuristics~\cite{ogb2026leaderboard}, and the
gap persists, so reporting a baseline does not by itself raise the bar. A headline within a few points of the
floor is hard to read as evidence of learning. Where a paper releases its code, the gap can be measured on
its own model: NIMGSA's~\cite{jin2022nimgsa} 0.937 and HybridGNN's~\cite{ahmad2026hybridgnn} 0.976 fall to
0.526 and 0.518, at the level of disease popularity, and HGDTI's~\cite{yu2022hgdti} 0.9995 to 0.573, under a
model-free floor of 0.740, once their leaks are removed and negatives are
degree-matched; NGCN's~\cite{cao2024ngcn} 0.924, which does not leak, falls to 0.671--0.753 with matched negatives
alone, against a floor of 0.725; kmerPMTF's~\cite{zhang2024kmerpmtf} 0.86--0.91 falls to 0.50--0.52; and
CoupleMDA's~\cite{li2025couplemda} 0.949, 0.3 points above miRNA degree as published, to 0.515--0.525, both under
their floors (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}).

\subsection{A minimum reporting standard and \texttt{protocheck}}
\label{sec:standard}
Given how directly the inflation problem responds to a small set of checks, we propose these as a minimum reporting standard for evaluation methodology in this subfield. First, a leak-free edge split, with an automated check that held-out edges are unreachable to the encoder during message passing. Second, matched negative sampling: negative-sampling distributions that are matched between training and evaluation rather than assumed compatible. Third, at least one model-free baseline reported alongside any trained model, \emph{and the margin over it stated}. Six of the surveyed papers report such a comparator and none reads its own margin. We release \texttt{protocheck}, a tool that verifies whether an evaluation meets the standard on the user's own splits. Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol} gives the three requirements and the check of \texttt{protocheck} that verifies each.

\input{tables/table5_correct_protocol_checklist.tex}

\texttt{protocheck} takes the train and held-out pairs of a link-prediction experiment and, optionally, the edges the encoder sees, the training and evaluation negatives and the model's AUROC. It reports each check as ok, warn or fail; a check whose input was not supplied is reported as unchecked, never as passed. When held-out edges reach the encoder, the floor also includes handing the encoder's input back unchanged, and the margin is never reported as ok. It needs only numpy, scipy and scikit-learn, and runs from the command line, from Python, or in a Colab notebook on the user's own files (Data availability). It also runs a fourth, diagnostic check for candidate columns that carry no positive anywhere (Section~\ref{sec:dead_columns}), which is not part of the standard. The tool inspects the experiment's data, not the model: the user runs it on the saved splits and negatives before reporting a number, and revises the split or the sampler if a check fails. It takes the supplied edges as given, so it shows that the files are consistent with the protocol, not that the user's code behaves as the files say. It cannot see leakage that does not pass through exact edges, such as node features derived from held-out labels, and it does not show that any published number is inflated. Its thresholds are heuristics, not validated cut-offs. The degree-profile check compares each distance with what sampling noise alone produces at the observed sample sizes, so a small test set is not flagged for histogram noise, and it warns outright when fewer than 30 held-out positives are given; from the command line, a run whose leakage check could not be performed exits with a non-zero status rather than as a pass.

We validated the implementation, not the claim. On the five HMDD-type graphs under the $2\times2$ grid,
where the true state of every cell is known by construction (four seeds, 80 audits), the leakage check
flagged all 40 audits with a leak and the negative-matching check all 40 with mismatched negatives, with no
false alarm in the 40 clean audits of either kind. On ogbl-ddi (one seed, four cells) both checks were
2/2 and OGB's official negatives triggered the matching warning, and the tool's heuristic scores match the
repository's own baseline to $7\times10^{-10}$ AUROC. Its model-free floor agrees with our earlier
measurement to within 0.011 AUROC on all five graphs (Welch $|z|\le1.7$), against gaps of about 0.3 that this
paper measures. The criterion we fixed in advance (within two standard deviations of the per-split spread)
failed on two graphs, DiGAMN and CoupleMDA, because it compared a four-split mean with a single-split
spread; we replaced it with the Welch comparison after seeing the failure. These results show that the
implementation reproduces our audit scripts. They do not show that its thresholds are right or that it finds
leakage beyond exact edges, no model was trained, so the margin check was exercised only as a floor
computation, and the empty-column check is not independently validated. An independent stress test with
synthetic cases built to break the tool found no leaked set reported as clean once the encoder's edges were
supplied; the false alarms it found on small test sets, and the zero exit status of an unchecked leakage
test, were fixed before the validation above was re-run, with unchanged detection counts; it was re-run again with version 0.2.0, with the same counts.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
(i) The cross-architecture grid is one run per model; the inflation is an order of magnitude above the seed
spread, but a repeated grid would strengthen it. (ii) Our heuristics are local; global ones (Katz \cite{katz1953status}, rooted
PageRank \cite{liben2007link}) may sit higher, so the 3.6-point margin is an upper bound on how far the model exceeds a floor.
(iii) None of the four trained models encodes pairwise structure as SEAL \cite{zhang2018seal}, NBFNet
\cite{zhu2021nbfnet} or BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} do, so 3.6 points is not a ceiling for trained models,
and whether a structure-aware architecture closes it is open. (iv) The six-graph experiment retrains our
architecture on each paper's graph, not each paper's model (NIMGSA's, HybridGNN's, HGDTI's, kmerPMTF's, NGCN's and CoupleMDA's released code are the
exceptions, Section~\ref{sec:generalization}), and six of the seven audited protocols are
reconstructed from the paper's matrix and stated negative ratio; it shows that the conventional protocol inflates
a trained model on those graphs, not that those papers' figures are inflated by that amount; a paper's own
figure we measured only for these six, NIMGSA's only approximately, since part of its similarity
input could not be rebuilt without the held-out miRNAs, HybridGNN's on released data that differ from
the dataset its paper describes, and HGDTI's with a model module rebuilt from bytecode, since only a compiled
one is released; NGCN's released model omits the graph convolution its paper describes, and kmerPMTF's spectral layer is run with the $K=2$ of its README rather than the $K=1$ of its text and code default (Table~\ref{tab:kmerpmtf_rerun} gives both), so in both what is measured is the released code. (v) The survey that
motivates the study is a two-rater instrument (Cohen's $\kappa$ 0.44 and 0.49 before adjudication) and is not
itself a result. (vi) The label source, miRDB, is itself a prediction model, so a better architecture would not
lift the information ceiling. (vii) Unlabeled pairs are not all true negatives: some are associations not yet
recorded, and they concentrate on popular columns, which is where degree-matched negatives are drawn. The
corrected protocol is therefore biased downward, and part of what we attribute to negative sampling is label
noise. We measured how much on the canonical HMDD matrix, the one graph whose source release (HMDD v2.0) has a
later one (v3.2) that its names can be matched against: v3.2 records 25\% of its degree-matched negatives as
associations, against 10\% of its uniform ones (21 seeds, on the 201 of 380 diseases whose names match).
Dropping those negatives lowers the heuristics' held-out drop from uniform to degree-matched negatives
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol_grid}) from 0.31 to 0.26, so the associations known today explain about a sixth of it.
This is a lower bound: v3.2 is itself incomplete, and the hidden positives it records score above the held-out
positives on average, so a larger share cannot be ruled out. On our own graph, whose labels are miRDB
predictions, it cannot be measured (\texttt{analysis/hmdd\_hidden\_positives.py}). We also leave unexplained why the margin over the floor widens under the corrected
protocol on the five external graphs but narrows on ours (Section~\ref{sec:collapse_general}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Under conventions common in this subfield, a trained link predictor scores AUROC 0.879--0.992 on six
biomedical interaction graphs. Correcting both conventions drops every one into 0.612--0.633, a loss of
0.26--0.38. Five of those graphs are other groups' published matrices, so the inflation is not a property of
one dataset. The two failures compound on our graph, negative sampling carries most of the loss on the five
surveyed graphs, part of it label noise, and candidate columns that can never be positive raise the model-free floor on their own.
Against the surveyed papers' own numbers, trained models beat that floor by 4.7 points on average and the
heuristic wins outright on MEAHNE's graph; rerun with their own released code, NIMGSA's 0.937 and
HybridGNN's 0.976 fall to 0.526 and 0.518, the level of disease popularity, and HGDTI's 0.9995 to 0.573, under
a 0.740 floor, NGCN's 0.924, without any leak, to 0.671--0.753 against 0.725, and kmerPMTF's 0.86--0.91 and CoupleMDA's 0.949 to 0.50--0.52, under their floors; on OGB the heuristic ranks 30th of 32 on ogbl-ddi and 14th of 27 on ogbl-ppa.
On our graph the inflation recurs across four trained models, a graph-free MLP included, and
cell typing survives the same audit by 32 points, so the effect follows the evaluation protocol, not the model.
Under the corrected protocol our model exceeds its model-free floor by 3.6 points.

We propose a minimum reporting standard with three requirements: a leak-free edge split, matched negative
sampling, and a model-free baseline with its margin stated. We release \texttt{protocheck}, an open-source
tool that verifies on a user's own splits and negatives whether an evaluation meets it, so that groups working
on biomedical interaction graphs can audit before reporting a headline number.

reported 0.80--0.91 are labelled AUROC in the paper's Table~2 and AUPRC in its text; the rerun's AUROC with the
released evaluation graph reproduces them. Evaluated over the training graph, the $K=2$ model scores at or under
chance on two species (0.396 on \emph{A.\ thaliana}), as one would expect of a model that learns to read the
query edge from its input and is then shown queries whose edge is absent; we did not test that explanation.

\section{\texttt{protocheck} on seven published evaluations}
\label{app:protocheck_cases}
\setcounter{table}{0}
All seven cases use \texttt{protocheck} 0.2.0 (\texttt{analysis/protocheck\_mgcnss.py},
\texttt{analysis/protocheck\_nimgsa.py}, and the audits inside \texttt{analysis/hybridgnn\_rerun.py},
\texttt{analysis/hgdti\_rerun.py}, \texttt{analysis/ngcn\_rerun.py}, \texttt{analysis/couplemda\_rerun.py} and
\texttt{analysis/kmerpmtf\_rerun.py});
Table~\ref{tab:protocheck_cases} gives every scenario fold by fold as ranges. Each scenario is what someone holding a given set of files could audit.

\paragraph{MGCNSS} Its repository ships five cross-validation folds of 1,086 held-out positives and 1,086
negatives each, and the precomputed network its model reads; the model builds its propagated adjacency from
that network's association matrix, so that matrix is the encoder's edge set. The fold files store positives
1-based and negatives 0-based, and must be read with separate index bases (\texttt{--neg-index-base}): read
with one base, 11--14 negatives per fold land on known associations and 1--9 fall outside the matrix; with
the right bases, none does. From the folds alone the tool warns that the evaluation negatives do not mirror
the positives' column degrees (TV 0.53--0.58, against a sampling-noise quantile of about 0.07), while
training and evaluation negatives match each other (TV 0.02--0.04), and it finds the reported 0.9874 6.3 to
9.1 points above a floor that sees only the training edges. With the network supplied it fails the leakage
check on every fold: all 1,086 held-out positives are in the encoder's input. The four topology heuristics,
seeing that same input, sit 2.6 to 4.1 points below 0.9874, but returning the input itself separates every
held-out positive from every negative (AUROC 1.000), so the margin is $-1.3$ points and the check fails.
Two choices behind the number are outside what the tool can see: the test negatives were picked by the
paper's own selector, of which it sees only the degree profile, and the public code keeps the epoch with the
best test AUROC, which is training code, not data.

\paragraph{NIMGSA} The paper ships no split files: its folds are generated by its cross-validation script,
which sets the rows of 20\% of the miRNAs to zero in the input matrix. They are rebuilt here with the same
random-number order as the rerun of \ref{app:nimgsa}, which reproduced the paper's 0.9354, and each
audit is given that fold's measured AUROC, so every margin is per fold. Training negatives are every zero of
the input matrix, since the reconstruction loss treats each as a zero target. Four scenarios follow, from what
a reader can check to the corrected protocol.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{As published, split only.} The script scores every positive of the matrix (5,430) against every
zero, so those are the held-out set and its negatives. The tool warns that the negatives do not mirror the
positives (TV 0.58, sampling-noise quantile 0.02) and finds the model 7.0 to 7.4 points above a disease's
training degree (0.865); leakage is unchecked.
\item \emph{As published, with the encoder's input.} Supplying the fold's input matrix fails the leakage check:
77--83\% of the scored positives are in it, because the training rows are scored too. Returning the input
then reaches 0.966--0.974, above the model in every fold (margin $-2.9$ to $-3.9$ points), and the margin
check fails. Version 0.1.0, whose leaked floor held only the four topology heuristics, had reported this
margin as ok at $+7$ points, because none of them scores a pair by its own edge; that is why 0.2.0 adds the
returned input to the floor.
\item \emph{Held-out rows only.} Scoring only the zeroed rows (913--1,225 positives per fold) against their
zeros passes the leakage check, keeps the negative warning (TV 0.54--0.59) and reports a margin of 6.1 to
8.8 points, as ok. This is the scenario the tool gets wrong: the miRNA similarity graph the model propagates
over is fixed across folds and 73.6\% a kernel of the full association matrix, so held-out associations reach
the model as a node feature. Rebuilding that kernel without the held-out rows lowers the model from 0.921 to
0.856, 0.4 points above the floor (Table~\ref{tab:nimgsa_rerun}).
\item \emph{Corrected.} With one degree-matched negative per held-out positive (no uniform fallback; 8--40
positives per fold took the nearest degree bin) and the AUROC of the model whose similarity was rebuilt, the
evaluation negatives now mirror the positives (TV 0.01--0.04, under the noise quantile), and the margin is
$-1.7$ to $+1.2$ points: a warning in three folds and a failure in two. The remaining warning is correct: the
model still trains on every zero and is scored on matched negatives (TV 0.54--0.58 between the two).
\end{enumerate}
The rebuilt similarity in the last scenario is not something the tool checks; it is supplied as the model's
measured AUROC. What the tool contributes there is the floor and the negative profile.

\paragraph{HybridGNN} Each of its folds is audited with the graph that fold's model propagates over as the
encoder's edges, on its released data (6,292--6,293 test positives per fold, 25 audits per step;
Table~\ref{tab:hybridgnn_rerun}). As released, the leakage check fails on every audit: all test positives
are in the graph, because it is built once from the full matrix. The negatives warn (TV 0.60--0.62), and
returning the input scores 1.000, so the model's 0.974--0.978 fails the margin check by 2.2 to 2.7 points.
With the graph and features built from training edges, leakage is ok and the negatives still warn; the
model clears the best heuristic (Adamic--Adar, 0.918--0.927) by only 0.0 to 1.3 points, a warning on every
audit. With degree-matched negatives the profile matches (TV 0.03--0.04) and the model sits 0.1 to 1.0 points
under disease degree, a failure on every audit; the remaining negative warning is again correct, since the
model trains on uniform negatives. The leak here runs through edges, so the tool sees it, but two parts of it
are features (the kernel similarity and the factorisation of the full matrix) that it cannot see; it flags the
evaluation because the graph leaks as well. The epoch kept by test AUROC is training code, outside its scope.

\paragraph{HGDTI} The paper ships no split files: its folds are drawn at run time, so each of the 30 folds of
the rerun (three seeds of ten, 192--193 test positives each; Table~\ref{tab:hgdti_rerun}) is audited with the
adjacency its model propagates over at test time as the encoder's edges. As released, the leakage check fails
on every audit: every test positive is in that adjacency, because it is built from the test labels. The
negatives warn (TV 0.63--0.75), the dead-candidate check warns (about 1,100 of 1,512 proteins have no training
positive, and 74\% of the test negatives sit on them), and returning the input scores 1.000, so the model's
0.998--1.000 fails the margin check; without the returned input the floor would be protein degree, 0.887.
Propagating over training edges, leakage is ok and the negatives and dead candidates still warn; the model
clears protein degree by $-0.5$ to $+9.2$ points (22 ok, 7 warn, 1 fail). With uniform test negatives it
sits 3.1 points under to 2.5 over that floor (17 fail, 13 warn). With degree-matched negatives the profile
matches (TV 0.00), the dead-candidate check drops to information (12\% of negatives on dead columns), and the
model fails the margin on every audit, 12.5 to 22.1 points under Adamic--Adar; the negative check now warns
for the other reason it can, training against evaluation negatives (TV 0.63--0.74), which the random-trained
arm of Table~\ref{tab:hgdti_rerun} confirms costs the model 9.0 points on that test. The leak runs through an
edge, so the tool sees it from the released pipeline alone; the reliable negatives, chosen with every
interaction, are a selection effect the tool reports only through their degree profile.

\paragraph{NGCN} Its split is drawn at run time as well; each seed's split is audited with the validation-selected
model (Table~\ref{tab:ngcn_rerun}, steps 2--3; 385 test positives per split), and since the model reads no
drug--target edge the encoder's edges are the training positives. The leakage check is ok on all ten audits
(five seeds, two ratios). With the released uniform negatives the negative check warns (TV 0.64--0.72), the
dead-candidate check warns (71--77\% of the test negatives on proteins without a training positive), and the
model clears protein degree by 2.1 to 5.6 points: ok on two audits, a warning on eight. With degree-matched
negatives the dead-candidate check drops to information and the floor becomes Adamic--Adar (0.715--0.733);
the negative check now warns because training and evaluation negatives differ (TV 0.65--0.71). Trained at 1:1
the model fails the margin on all five audits ($-7.4$ to $-2.7$ points); trained at 1:10 it clears it by 0.4
to 4.4, a warning on all five. No check flags a leak because there is none; what the tool reports is an easy
test.

\paragraph{CoupleMDA} Its test set is fixed, so each seed's model is audited on the same 1,523 test positives
with the training graph as the encoder's edges, in both orientations (miRNAs as rows and as columns, so that the
column-degree checks cover either side), reporting the one with the higher floor. The leakage check fails on
every audit, for the 42 test positives (2.8\%) that are also training edges; with so few, returning the input
scores only what its miRNA-degree tie-breaker scores. With the released negatives, the check of negatives against
positives passes with diseases as columns, because the sampler keeps the disease, and warns with miRNAs as
columns (TV 0.77--0.79), which is where the floor sits (0.942--0.948); the model clears it by 0.1 to 0.4 points, a
warning, since a margin over a leaked floor is not read as evidence of learning. With uniform negatives the
dead-candidate check warns (55\% of the negatives on empty disease columns) and the model fails the margin by 1.4
to 3.2 points. With negatives matched on either side, the profile matches on that side, the remaining warning is
again training against evaluation negatives (TV 0.78--0.79), and the model fails the margin by 1.2 to 2.9 points.
The leak here is real but small; what drives the number is a negative sampler that matches one side's popularity
and not the other's, which the tool sees only when the free side is put in the columns.

\paragraph{kmerPMTF} Its folds are drawn at run time; each of the 60 folds (four species, three seeds, five folds;
$K=2$) is audited with the miRNA--gene edges of the graph the spectral layer propagates over at test time as the
encoder's edges. As released, the leakage check fails on every audit: every test positive is an edge of the
evaluation graph, and returning the input scores 1.000, 6.9 to 18.7 points above the model. Over the training
graph, leakage is ok, the negatives warn (TV 0.31--0.61), and the model fails the margin against common
neighbours or Adamic--Adar on 53 audits and warns on 7, all on tomato. With degree-matched negatives it fails on all
60, by 3.8 to 15.4 points. Against every remaining unlabeled pair, the dead-candidate check warns (most such
pairs fall on genes without a positive) and the model clears preferential attachment or gene degree by 4.7 to
19.6 points (53 ok, 7 warn): the one scenario of the seven in which a leak-free evaluation leaves a published
model well above its floor, on unmatched negatives that the paper describes but its code does not use.
