\begin{abstract}
Rigorous data handling and transparent evaluation reporting are foundational to reliable network biology, yet
Graph Neural Network (GNN) papers predicting microRNA--target interactions routinely
report AUROC in the 0.91--0.99 range without establishing model free baselines. 
A literature audit of 22 such papers found that none
report a model-free baseline and 16 of 22 treat every unlabeled pair as a
uniformly-sampled negative. We quantify the combined, previously unmeasured cost of
these two evaluation pitfalls on a real biomedical interaction graph. We built a
heterogeneous graph from a public single-cell RNA-seq cohort and miRDB v6.0
miRNA--target interactions, training a heterogeneous graph transformer to score
miRNA--gene pairs. We then compared a conventional evaluation protocol against one that
corrects both message-passing edge leakage and negative-sampling bias, across six
architectures and a set of model-free baselines. The conventional protocol (edges seen
during message passing, uniform negatives) reports AUROC 0.9867 $\pm$ 0.0011. 
Correcting both flaws simultaneously drops this to 0.6276 $\pm$
0.0070, within 3.6 points of a one-line, no-learning topological heuristic
(Adamic/Adar, 0.5912); the inflation is super-additive, so correcting either flaw
alone understates the total. Across six architectures, including a graph-free MLP,
every trained model inflates by 0.24--0.31 under the conventional protocol while an
untrained control does not move. A structurally similar but genuinely unaffected task
in the same pipeline, cell-type classification, reaches 0.9916 accuracy and survives an
analogous no-graph control by a 32-point margin (best no-graph baseline 0.6692),
confirming the audit is discriminating rather than indiscriminate. Applying the same
model-free heuristics directly to seven of the surveyed papers' own miRNA-disease
datasets, independently obtained, the trained models beat the topology floor by 4.7
AUROC points on average, but the gap reverses on the two sparsest graphs audited -- on
one, the topology-only heuristic outright beats the paper's own trained model. The
inflation is a property
of the evaluation protocol, not of any one architecture or dataset, and this subfield's own
reporting practice does not check for it. We propose a minimum reporting standard: an
automated leak-free split check and at least one model-free baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Mapping the complex web of biomolecule-biomolecule interactions, particularly microRNA
and drug targets, is essential for advancing modern network biology
\cite{barabasi2004networkbiology,vidal2011interactome}. However,
despite the biological complexity of these relationships,
Graph Neural Network papers predict an AUROC in the
0.91--0.99 range \cite{tian2024mgcnss,jin2022nimgsa,ahmad2026hybridgnn,yu2022hgdti,
cao2024ngcn,zhang2024kmerpmtf,almusawi2023review,orro2026mirnadisease,
li2025couplemda,zeng2025gonnmda,liu2024digamn,lu2024dgnmda,yu2022hlgnnmda,
huang2022meahne,li2022cksnpgnn,liang2023hmcda,wang2026higldp,xiong2025gpsdti,
zhang2025saegraphdti,yang2025dtimhapr,sun2024ingnndti,jia2024modulepred}. A
literature audit of these 22 papers found that none report a model-free baseline, and
that 16 of the 22 default to uniformly-sampled negatives without discussion (protocol
in Methods, full results in Related Work below). Neither omission is novel: both are
already documented pitfalls in the broader link-prediction and knowledge-graph
literature, reviewed below. What is missing is the connection between that general
literature and this subfield's own reporting practice -- the general-ML literature
already knows how to check for these pitfalls, but this subfield's papers do not apply
the check. Concretely, this paper's contribution is fourfold: it quantifies what that
gap costs on a real biomedical interaction graph, shows that the two pitfalls compound
rather than simply add, shows the cost recurs across independently trained
architectures rather than being an artifact of any single model, and shows the same
model-free floor is high enough to matter -- on two of seven independently obtained
datasets, decisive -- when measured directly on other papers' own data rather than
only inferred by analogy.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Known evaluation pitfalls in link prediction}
Neither failure mode we quantify in this paper is novel to machine learning in general.
Transductive, seen-edge leakage traces at least to the inverse-relation leak documented
in the FB15k knowledge-graph benchmark \cite{toutanova2015observed}, and is actively
being re-examined in the broader link-prediction literature
\cite{zhu2023pitfalls,li2023evaluating}. The same failure recurs closer to this paper's
own domain: a leakage problem inflating reported performance has been independently
identified in miRNA--disease association prediction \cite{dong2020mirnaleakage}, and,
contemporaneously, train/test redundancy has been shown to inflate knowledge-graph-embedding
link prediction across several biomedical graphs -- though that study's own permutation
test found no evidence its models exploit node degree specifically, a useful contrast to
the degree-driven bias we quantify below \cite{briere2026kgeleakage}. Popularity-biased negative sampling is a
known, quantified bias in link prediction generally \cite{kotnis2017analysis,
aiyappa2024implicit}, and has been documented specifically in network biology
\cite{yilmaz2025biasaware}, the domain this paper's graph is drawn from. Its likely
historical root is recommender-systems evaluation practice, where sampling a small set
of negatives per positive was adopted for computational convenience and only later
shown to distort ranking metrics \cite{krichene2020sampled}.

\subsection{How this subfield evaluates itself}
Against that backdrop, we surveyed 22 papers in the miRNA-target and adjacent
biomolecule-interaction link-prediction literature (miRNA-disease, circRNA-disease,
lncRNA-disease, drug-target, and gene-disease), reading each paper's methods section in
full rather than inferring its evaluation protocol from the abstract. None of the 22
report a model-free baseline. 16 of the 22 treat every unlabeled pair as a
uniformly-sampled negative by default, without discussing the alternative; among the
papers in the original seven-paper pilot that deviate from uniform sampling, two
(HybridGNN, kmerPMTF) make a better negative-selection strategy their own headline
contribution -- an implicit admission that the default is known to be inadequate. Whether
held-out edges are removed from the encoder's input is unclear from the methods section
in 12 of the 22, up from 3 of the original 7; this reporting gap, not confirmed leakage,
is now the dominant finding on that dimension. Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}
summarizes the full sample.

We deliberately do not claim that this subfield routinely leaks edges: only 2 of the 22
papers surveyed clearly do, 7 clearly strip test edges correctly, and our own project's
original split was worse than the norm we observed. The claim we make is narrower, and
we argue more damaging precisely because it is universal: no paper in the sample checks
itself against a model-free control. Getting the split right is not protective on its
own -- the two papers from the original pilot that do strip held-out edges from the
graph, HybridGNN and kmerPMTF, still draw negatives from unlabeled pairs, one uniformly
at random and the other count-matched rather than degree-matched, and neither reports a
model-free baseline (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}). The two failure modes are
independent: fixing the split is not evidence against the popularity shortcut this
paper identifies, which is precisely why, as Results shows, correcting both is
super-additive rather than redundant.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data and graph construction}
We build a heterogeneous graph from a public single-cell RNA-seq cohort combined with
miRDB v6.0 miRNA--target interactions. The graph has three node types -- miRNA, gene,
and cell -- connected by a miRNA-to-gene regulatory edge, a cell-to-gene expression
edge, and a gene-to-gene co-expression edge, each with an automatically-added reverse
relation for bidirectional message passing (Figure~\ref{fig:graph_schema}). The
disease/cohort label attached to each cell (case vs.\ control) is retained purely as
data provenance: it never enters the graph structure, the model input, the training
objective, or any scoring head, so nothing in this paper's predictions can depend on
it. During preparation of this study we identified and fixed two bugs in the
graph-construction pipeline, in co-expression gene selection and in the cell-to-gene
expression threshold; every headline number reported here was re-measured on the
corrected graph, independently verified, and shown to replicate the pre-fix figures
within one standard deviation.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
We use \emph{transductive evaluation} to name the specific condition audited here: a
held-out edge is left in the message-passing graph when its score is computed, so the
encoder can, in effect, observe the exact edge it is later asked to predict. This is
narrower than the general transductive/inductive distinction in graph learning, which
concerns whether test-time nodes are visible during training and is not by itself a
source of leakage. We split edges with \texttt{RandomLinkSplit} and strip each held-out edge, together
with its automatically-added reverse relation, from the message-passing graph in both
directions, so that no test edge is reachable to the encoder at evaluation time. This
leak-free property is not assumed: it is verified by an automated battery of checks
before any downstream number is trusted (Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split}). The same
hide-and-recover logic is also applied within the training set itself: a fraction of
the training edges are reserved as supervision targets only and withheld from the
encoder's own input, so the model cannot simply read a training edge off the graph
while being asked to predict it. For negative
sampling we compare two regimes, uniform-random and degree-matched, and enforce the
same sampler at training and at evaluation time within any single reported number.
Mixing the two -- training under one regime and evaluating under the other -- produces
what we call the ``mismatch trap'': an apparent difficulty measurement that is in fact
an artifact of the distribution mismatch, which we report as a methodological warning
in its own right (Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}). Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol}
summarizes these two requirements, together with the model-free-baseline requirement
described below, as a minimum reporting checklist. We report AUROC and AUPRC for link
prediction, and accuracy and macro-F1 for cell typing. Headline numbers are averaged
over four independent random seeds where feasible; the six-architecture grid is
measured from a single training run per architecture, a choice we justify in Results by
effect size against the seed-to-seed variability observed elsewhere.

\subsection{Baselines}
For link prediction we report four no-learning topological heuristics -- gene
in-degree, preferential attachment, common neighbours, and Adamic--Adar -- each
computed purely from training-visible edge topology. For cell typing we report two
no-learning or graph-free controls, nearest-centroid and logistic regression, both
trained directly on the raw PCA features without any message passing. Against these
controls we compare six trained architectures: a heterogeneous graph transformer, a
homogeneous GCN, a co-expression-ablation variant of the transformer, a graph-free MLP,
an untrained random-weight control, and a no-miRNA-input ablation that is
link-prediction-incapable by construction and retained only as a smoke test.

\subsection{Literature survey protocol}
The literature survey referenced throughout this paper covers 22 papers, each read in
full at the methods-section level rather than judged from its abstract, and classified
along four dimensions: whether it cross-validates over edges, whether it removes
held-out edges from the encoder's input, how it selects negative examples, and whether
it reports any model-free baseline. This is an expansion, completed 2026-08-12, of an
initial seven-paper pilot; one limitation remains open by construction -- every
classification was made by a single rater, so the "unclear" calls in
Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey} have not been cross-checked by a second independent
reader, and we report that as a limitation (Discussion) rather than resolving it here. A
2026-08-13 re-read of the same rater's own "unclear" classifications against each paper's
primary source, undertaken to check whether "unclear" reflected a genuine reporting gap
or overly conservative extraction, corrected 3 of 14 spot-checked cells; the tallies
reported throughout this paper reflect those corrections (see Discussion).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Quantifying the inflation}
Under the conventional evaluation protocol -- edges visible to the encoder during
message passing, negatives drawn uniformly at random -- the trained heterogeneous graph
transformer reaches a test AUROC of 0.9867 $\pm$ 0.0011, averaged across four
independent random seeds. Correcting both flaws simultaneously -- holding out test edges
from message passing and degree-matching negatives to positives -- drops this to 0.6276
$\pm$ 0.0070 (AUPRC 0.6598 $\pm$ 0.0049). Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} and
Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid} report the full 2$\times$2 grid crossing both corrections
independently: fixing either flaw alone still leaves a substantially inflated number,
and only correcting both reaches the lower figure, confirming that the two corrections
are super-additive rather than redundant (the exact per-term attribution is
convention-sensitive, as discussed in Methods, so we report the total rather than a
decomposition). This result is not an artifact of a single snapshot of the graph: an
independently applied fix to the graph-construction pipeline reproduces both the
inflated and the corrected figures within 0.1 standard deviations, and the
topology-only floor reported below is numerically identical before and after that fix,
since it touched only co-expression and cell-expression edges, not the miRNA-to-gene
topology the floor is computed from.

\subsection{Model-free controls}
Model-free controls place the corrected trained model close to a purely topological
floor. The best no-learning heuristic, Adamic--Adar, reaches an AUROC of 0.5912 under
the corrected protocol, within 3.6 points of the trained heterogeneous graph
transformer (0.6276); Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines} reports all four topological
heuristics against both negative-sampling regimes. The direction reverses under the
conventional protocol: a heuristic that never looks at the miRNA at all, gene
in-degree, reaches 0.8712 and outperforms the trained model (0.8056). The mechanism
behind this reversal is directly testable: a model trained with uniform negatives and
then evaluated against degree-matched negatives collapses toward chance, AUROC 0.5352
$\pm$ 0.0202, indistinguishable from the gene-degree heuristic's floor -- direct
evidence that uniform-negative training teaches the model to exploit gene popularity
rather than miRNA-specific target information. Figure~\ref{fig:negative_sampler_collapse}
shows this mismatch-induced collapse is not specific to the heterogeneous transformer:
it recurs across every trained architecture in the grid.

\subsection{The inflation generalizes across architectures}
Extending the protocol grid across six independently trained architectures -- the
heterogeneous graph transformer, a homogeneous GCN, a co-expression ablation, a
graph-free MLP, an untrained random-weight control, and a no-miRNA-input ablation
retained as a smoke test -- shows the inflation is not a property of any single model.
Every trained architecture inflates by 0.24--0.31 AUROC points between the conventional
and corrected protocols, including the graph-free MLP, which never sees the graph
topology at all; the untrained control, by contrast, does not move ($-0.004$). Each
architecture here is measured from a single training run rather than averaged across
seeds, but the inflation magnitude is an order of magnitude larger than the
seed-to-seed spread measured elsewhere in this study (0.005--0.02 AUROC points), so the
qualitative pattern does not depend on averaging away noise. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_inflation}
plots the full comparison, and Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid} gives the numeric
grid, including the no-miRNA-input smoke test. A secondary finding emerges from the
same grid: under the corrected protocol, a plain homogeneous GCN (0.6236) outperforms
the heterogeneous graph transformer (0.6080) -- the added architectural complexity this
project was originally built around does not earn its keep even against the simplest
graph-based baseline.

\subsection{The inflation is not specific to our own graph}
The literature survey (Section~\ref{sec:related}) shows this subfield never reports a
model-free control, but stops short of measuring what that control would show on the
surveyed papers' own data -- it argues by analogy to our graph. We closed that gap for
seven miRNA-disease papers from Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey} whose data could be
independently obtained: their own bundled repositories, or a shared institutional
mirror for one (CKSNP-GNN), each verified against the paper's own reported node/edge
counts before use (three further papers -- GONNMDA, Orro, DGNMDA -- were excluded
because no usable data was recoverable; see Discussion). Applying the same topology-only
scorers from Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}, unmodified, to each paper's own
association matrix under uniform-random negatives at that paper's stated ratio
(Table~\ref{tab:hmdd_survey_audit}), the trained models beat the topology floor by 4.7
AUROC points on average -- a real, independently-measured gap, not merely our own
graph's finding restated. But the gap is highly graph-dependent, ranging from $-9.2$
points (DiGAMN) to $+3.3$ points: on MEAHNE's graph, the topology-only heuristic
outright \emph{beats} the paper's own trained model (0.9848 vs.\ 0.9520), and on
CoupleMDA's the two are within 1.3 points. Sanity-checking with degree-matched
negatives collapses every heuristic on every paper to 0.55--0.58, confirming the
uniform-negative numbers reflect a genuine popularity ceiling rather than a data error.
The two papers where the trained model adds the least are also the two sparsest graphs
audited (MEAHNE: 17{,}972 edges over 1{,}296 $\times$ 11{,}783 nodes; CoupleMDA:
$\sim$15{,}000 edges over 2{,}090 $\times$ 1{,}754 nodes), consistent with a mechanism
in which a sparser graph makes popularity alone a harder ceiling for a trained model to
clear -- a testable hypothesis for future work, not a claim we verify further here. We
do not conclude that these five other trained models learned nothing: five of seven
still clearly separate from the topology floor. The claim is narrower and already
sufficient: \emph{the popularity floor this literature never checks for is frequently
high enough to matter, and on some of its own graphs, decisive.}

\subsection{A control task that survives its own audit}
To check that this audit methodology does not simply collapse every number in the
project, we apply the same no-learning-control logic to a structurally different task
trained on the same graph and pipeline: cell-type classification, which reaches 0.9916
test accuracy. This task carries a circularity risk analogous to the miRNA-target
task's own dependence on miRDB -- labels are the argmax of marker-gene scores computed
from expression, and the model's only cell-level feature is a PCA projection of that
same expression matrix. Two no-graph controls trained on the raw PCA features alone,
nearest-centroid and logistic regression, reach 0.4654 and 0.6692 respectively, a
32-point gap to the trained model's accuracy -- an order of magnitude larger than the
3.6-point gap the corrected link-prediction protocol leaves between the trained model
and its topological floor. Table~\ref{tab:celltype_control} reports both controls
against the reference model. This task survives the audit: the graph and message
passing are doing substantial, non-trivial work here, and the same methodology that
collapses the link-prediction headline leaves this one intact, confirming that the
audit is discriminating rather than indiscriminately deflating every reported number in
the project.

\subsection{Architectural limits on context-specific claims}
A brief methodological note follows from the same audit logic, though it is not itself
a headline result. Any claim that an interaction is predicted in a context-specific way
-- for example, cell-type-specific regulation -- should be checked against three
questions before it is trusted: whether the context variable is present in the
training data at all, whether the scoring head receives it as an input, and whether the
reported evaluation actually varies with it. Figure~\ref{fig:target_predictor} makes
the point concrete for the scoring head used throughout this paper: it takes only the
concatenated miRNA and gene embeddings and returns a single logit per pair, with no
architectural path for a cell embedding to enter the computation. The score for a given
miRNA--gene pair is therefore identical across every cell type by construction, and no
amount of retraining changes that without a different head architecture. We do not
attach any disease- or cell-type-specific narrative to this project's results; this
note is included as a generalizable check for evaluating such claims, not as a claim of
our own.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Each half of the inflation we measure already has a name in the broader link-prediction
literature, and neither is specific to microRNAs. The edge-split half is an instance of
what \cite{zhu2023pitfalls} call \emph{target-link inclusion} -- the target edge remains
reachable to the encoder through message passing at evaluation time, so the model can, in
effect, recover the label from the neighborhood it was never supposed to have. Its
lineage traces to the WN18/FB15k inverse-relation leak documented by
\cite{toutanova2015observed}, which was serious enough to motivate a replacement
benchmark (FB15k-237) rather than a footnote; Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split} is the same
mechanism restated for a bipartite miRNA--gene graph with explicit reverse relations
in place of duplicated inverse triples. The negative-sampling half is \emph{degree bias}
(also \emph{popularity bias}): \cite{aiyappa2024implicit} name and quantify it generally,
and \cite{yilmaz2025biasaware} document the identical pattern in network biology
specifically, the domain this graph is drawn from. Mechanistically, degree bias is a
textbook case of \emph{shortcut learning} \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}: a model trained
under uniform negatives has no incentive to learn miRNA-specific regulatory signal when
gene in-degree alone -- a feature that does not even require knowing which miRNA is
being scored -- separates positives from negatives almost for free
(Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}); collapsing to chance the moment that shortcut
is closed off (Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}) is the signature of a model that
learned the shortcut and nothing else, not of a harder evaluation. The historical root of
treating unlabeled pairs as negatives at all traces to sampled-metric evaluation practice
in recommender systems \cite{krichene2020sampled}, a convention that migrated into link
prediction without a matching re-derivation of when it is and is not valid.

What we have not found named in the literature is the interaction between the two: every
prior treatment we are aware of studies target-link inclusion or degree bias in
isolation. Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid} and Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} show they are
super-additive on this graph -- correcting either alone leaves the number substantially
inflated, and the cross-architecture grid (Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid},
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_inflation}) shows the same compounding recurs across six
independently-trained architectures, including a graph-free MLP, with the untrained
control the only one that does not move. We propose calling this \emph{compounded
evaluation inflation} to distinguish it from either pitfall reported alone: the
practical implication is that auditing a paper for target-link inclusion \emph{or} for a
model-free baseline, but not both, can still miss a substantially inflated headline
number.

The weakness of model-free baselines relative to trained models is not unique to our
own graph. Topological heuristics have been shown competitive with, or superior to,
trained neural link predictors elsewhere in the literature: on realistic biomedical
graphs, a plain Common Neighbours baseline holds its own against graph-embedding neural
methods on small or sparse, highly-disconnected graphs \cite{crichton2018realistic}, and
a purely topology-centric method has been shown to outperform state-of-the-art GNN link
predictors in accuracy while requiring a fraction of the training and inference cost
\cite{huang2023gelato}. On two independent, public link-prediction benchmarks from different
biological domains -- ogbl-ddi (drug--drug interaction) and ogbl-ppa
(protein--protein association), both verified leak-free at runtime by the same check
used throughout this paper -- the model-free heuristics from Methods reproduce the
Open Graph Benchmark's own published leaderboard numbers exactly. On ogbl-ppa,
Resource Allocation, a zero-parameter formula, ranks 14th of 27 leaderboard entries
(Hits@100 0.4933), essentially tied with the next-best entry, the trained model
BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} (0.4934), and outperforms the eleven trained or
learned methods ranked below it --
including canonical graph neural network architectures such as GCN (0.1867) and
GraphSAGE (0.1655), the same architecture family compared in our own cross-architecture
grid. Unlike the papers in our literature survey, OGB already reports these
heuristics alongside trained submissions -- exactly the practice we recommend -- yet a
formula with no learned parameters still holds its own against a substantial fraction
of the trained methods evaluated against it, suggesting the gap is not only that
model-free baselines go unreported, but that reporting one does not, by itself, raise
the bar the field treats as meaningful progress.

Six limitations qualify these results. First, the cross-architecture grid reports a
single measurement per architecture rather than an average over multiple training
runs; we consider this justified because the inflation effect size is far larger than
the run-to-run variability established elsewhere in this study, but a fully repeated
version of the grid would still strengthen the claim. Second, the task's information
ceiling is bounded by what the graph and its labels can support: the graph itself
carries no miRNA or gene sequence information, and the interaction label source, miRDB,
is itself the output of a seed-complementarity prediction model rather than an
experimentally confirmed interaction. This ceiling would not be resolved by a better
architecture; it would require predicting \emph{experimentally validated} interactions
-- for example, from miRTarBase -- instead of another predictor's output. Third, the
model-free baselines reported here are local neighborhood-overlap heuristics -- gene
degree, preferential attachment, common neighbours, and Adamic--Adar -- computed only
from the miRNA-to-gene adjacency; we did not evaluate global, path-integrating
heuristics such as the Katz index or rooted PageRank, which are known to outperform
local heuristics on sparse graphs and which the trained model's multi-hop,
multi-relation message passing more closely resembles. The 3.6-point gap between the
best local heuristic and the trained model should therefore be read as an upper bound
on how much the model exceeds a no-learning topological floor, not a tight one. Fourth,
the six trained architectures compared here (Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid}) are all
either generic message-passing networks (the project HGT, a homogeneous GCN) or a
graph-free MLP; none explicitly encodes pairwise structural signal -- shared neighbors,
enclosing-subgraph structure, or path distance -- the way link-prediction-specific
architectures such as SEAL \cite{zhang2018seal}, NBFNet \cite{zhu2021nbfnet}, Neo-GNN
\cite{yun2021neognn}, or BUDDY \cite{chamberlain2023buddy} do. Architectures of that
family place above local heuristics on properly-audited external leaderboards,
including the OGB benchmarks discussed above, where BUDDY narrowly outperforms
Resource Allocation on ogbl-ppa. The 3.6-point gap measured here should therefore not be read as a ceiling on
what any trained model could achieve on this graph under the corrected protocol -- only
on what the generic message-passing architectures tested here achieve; whether a
structure-aware architecture closes it is an open question this study does not
answer. Fifth, the 22-paper literature survey (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}) was
classified by a single rater; the "unclear" calls in particular -- 12 of 22 on whether
held-out edges are removed from the encoder's input -- have not been cross-checked by a
second independent reader, so that specific fraction should be read as this rater's
judgment rather than an inter-rater-agreed figure. A same-rater re-read of 14 of the
"unclear" cells against primary sources (2026-08-13) corrected 3, a 21\% rate that
suggests an independent second rater could still move the exact fraction, even though
it did not change which category is dominant. The two universal findings the
survey's argument rests on (zero of 22 papers report a model-free baseline; the split
status is frequently unrecoverable from the methods section as written) do not depend
on resolving any individual "unclear" call and are therefore more robust to this
limitation than the exact fraction is. Sixth, the seven-paper topology audit
(Table~\ref{tab:hmdd_survey_audit}) reconstructs, rather than reproduces, six of seven
papers' evaluation protocols: only MGCNSS's split and CoupleMDA's positive edges are
each paper's own exact data, the rest use an exact association matrix (verified against
each paper's own reported node/edge counts) under a self-generated split at the paper's
stated negative ratio, since no further split file was published. Three further
candidate papers from the same survey cluster (GONNMDA, Orro, DGNMDA) were excluded
from the audit entirely rather than approximated further: GONNMDA's linked repository
contains no code or data, Orro's data-availability statement provides no public
repository, and DGNMDA's cited data source was unreachable at the time of this audit.
The audit's headline number -- a 4.7-point average gap that reverses sign on two of
seven papers -- should be read as a lower-confidence, but independently-sourced,
complement to the single-graph result in Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}, not a
literal replication of any individual paper's reported figure.

Given how directly the inflation problem responds to a small set of checks, we propose
these as a minimum reporting standard for evaluation methodology in this subfield: an
automated, leak-free split check that verifies held-out edges are unreachable to the
encoder during message passing; at least one model-free baseline reported alongside any
trained model; and negative-sampling distributions that are matched between training
and evaluation rather than assumed compatible (summarized as a checklist in
Table~\ref{tab:correct_protocol}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On a real biomedical interaction graph, evaluating a heterogeneous graph transformer
under conventions common in this subfield -- edges visible to the encoder during
message passing, negatives sampled uniformly at random -- reports AUROC 0.9867.
Correcting both conventions simultaneously drops this to 0.6276, within 3.6 points of a
one-line, no-learning topological heuristic; the two corrections are super-additive, so
auditing for either pitfall alone is not sufficient to catch the inflation. The same
compounding recurs across six independently trained architectures, including a
graph-free MLP, while an untrained control does not move, and a structurally similar
but genuinely unaffected cell-typing control task survives the identical audit by a
wide margin -- together showing that the inflation is a property of the evaluation
protocol itself, not of any one architecture, and that the audit is discriminating
rather than indiscriminate. Beyond this specific graph, we contribute a reusable
methodology: an automated leak-free split check, model-free baselines for both link
prediction and node classification, and a cross-architecture protocol grid, released
alongside this paper so that other groups working on biomedical interaction graphs can
run the same audit on their own models before reporting a headline number.
