\begin{abstract}
\parttitle{Background}
Graph neural network (GNN) papers predicting microRNA--target (or, more broadly,
drug--target and biomolecule--biomolecule) interactions routinely report AUROC in the
0.91--0.99 range. A pilot audit of seven such papers in this literature found that none
report a model-free (no-learning) baseline, and that four of seven treat every unlabeled
pair as a uniformly-sampled negative by default. Neither convention is unique to this
literature: transductive train/message-passing leakage and popularity-biased negative
sampling are independently documented pitfalls in the broader knowledge-graph and
link-prediction literature \cite{toutanova2015observed,zhu2023pitfalls,li2023evaluating,
kotnis2017analysis,aiyappa2024implicit}, but their combined, quantified impact had not
been measured on a real biomedical interaction graph, and the fix that the broader ML
literature already knows had not reached this subfield's own reporting practice.

\parttitle{Results}
On a heterogeneous graph built from a public single-cell RNA-seq cohort and miRDB v6.0
miRNA--target interactions, we show that the conventional protocol -- evaluating on
edges visible during message passing, against uniformly-sampled negative pairs --
reports AUROC 0.9867 $\pm$ 0.0011 (4 seeds, test set). Correcting both flaws
simultaneously -- holding out edges from the message-passing graph and degree-matching
negatives to positives -- drops this to 0.6276 $\pm$ 0.0070, within 3.6 points of a
one-line, no-learning topological heuristic (Adamic--Adar, 0.5912). The $\approx$0.36
AUROC inflation is super-additive: correcting either flaw alone understates the total.
Re-running the full protocol grid across six architectures, including a graph-free MLP,
shows every trained model inflates by 0.24--0.31 under the conventional protocol while
an untrained control does not move -- the inflation is a property of the evaluation
protocol, not of any one architecture. A genuinely unaffected task in the same pipeline
(cell-type classification from the same graph, 0.9916 test accuracy) survives an
analogous no-learning control by a wide margin (best graph-free baseline 0.6692),
demonstrating the audit does not simply indict every number in the project.

\section*{Introduction}
\begin{itemize}
\item The claim in this literature: GNN-based miRNA-target / DTI link prediction papers
routinely report AUROC 0.91--0.99. A pilot literature audit (n=7, methods sections read
in full; protocol in Data and Methods, full results in Literature Review below) found
0/7 report any model-free baseline and 4/7 default to uniformly-sampled negatives for
unlabeled pairs -- source: \texttt{results/LITERATURE\_SURVEY.md}.
\item The gap this paper fills: the general-ML literature already knows the fix for
these evaluation pitfalls (Literature Review below); this subfield's own papers do not
apply it. We quantify what that gap costs on a real biomedical graph, and show the cost
generalizes across architectures rather than being one model's failure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Literature Review}

\subsection*{Known evaluation pitfalls in link prediction}
\begin{itemize}
\item Neither failure mode we quantify is novel to ML in general: transductive/seen-edge
leakage traces at least to the FB15k inverse-relation leak \cite{toutanova2015observed}
and is actively being re-examined \cite{zhu2023pitfalls,li2023evaluating};
popularity-biased negative sampling is a known, quantified bias in link prediction
generally \cite{kotnis2017analysis,aiyappa2024implicit} and specifically in network
biology \cite{yilmaz2025biasaware}, with likely historical roots in recommender-systems
evaluation practice \cite{krichene2020sampled}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{How this subfield evaluates itself}
\begin{itemize}
\item Pilot survey, n=7 papers, methods sections read in full (not inferred from
abstracts): 0/7 report a model-free baseline; 4/7 treat unlabeled pairs as uniform
negatives by default (two of the remaining three make better negative selection their
own headline contribution); split-leakage status is unclear in 3/7 papers because the
methods section does not say whether held-out edges reach the encoder.
\item We deliberately do \emph{not} claim the field routinely leaks edges -- 2/7 papers
in the pilot get the split right, and our own original split was worse than the norm.
The universal, defensible claim is narrower and, we argue, more damaging: no paper
checks itself against a model-free control. Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}
summarizes the pilot.
\item Getting the split right is not protective on its own: the two papers that do
strip held-out edges from the graph, HybridGNN and kmerPMTF, still draw negatives from
unlabeled pairs (uniform-random and count-matched, respectively) and neither reports a
model-free baseline (Table~\ref{tab:literature_survey}). The two failure modes are
independent -- fixing the split is not evidence against the popularity shortcut this
paper identifies, which is precisely why the two corrections are super-additive rather
than redundant.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Evaluation protocol}
\begin{itemize}
\item [STAGE] Edge split: \texttt{RandomLinkSplit} with reverse-relation stripping
(held-out edges removed from message passing in both directions), verified leak-free by
an automated 8-check test (\texttt{training/test\_edge\_split.py}) before any number is
trusted (Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split}).
\item [STAGE] Negative sampling: uniform-random vs. degree-matched, with the same
sampler enforced at training and evaluation time -- report the "mismatch trap" finding
(evaluating a matched-negative-trained model against uniform negatives produces an
artifact, not a difficulty measurement) as a methodological warning in its own right
(Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}).
\item [STAGE] Metrics: AUROC, AUPRC for link prediction; accuracy and macro-F1 for cell
typing. Statistical protocol: 4 seeds \{123, 777, 2024, 7\} where feasible; $n=1$ (seed
42) explicitly flagged for the six-architecture grid, justified by effect size.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Quantifying the inflation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Conventional protocol (edges seen during message passing, uniform negatives):
AUROC 0.9867 $\pm$ 0.0011, test set, 4 seeds \{123, 777, 2024, 7\},
\texttt{graphs\_v3fixed} (independently verified graph construction).
\item Corrected protocol (held-out edges verified leak-free by an automated 8-check test,
degree-matched negatives): AUROC 0.6276 $\pm$ 0.0070. AUPRC 0.6598 $\pm$ 0.0049.
Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} and Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid} give the full
2$\times$2 grid (both negative-sampling regimes crossed with both edge splits).
\item The two corrections are super-additive: fixing either one alone leaves a
substantially inflated number; only fixing both reaches the corrected figure. (Exact
per-term attribution is convention-sensitive -- see Data and Methods -- report the total, not
the split.)
\item Result replicates across an independent graph-construction bug fix: pre-fix and
post-fix graphs agree within 0.1$\sigma$, and the topology-only floor (below) is
bit-for-bit identical between the two, since the fix touched co-expression/expression
edges, not miRNA$\to$gene topology.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Model-free controls}
\begin{itemize}
\item Best no-learning topological heuristic (Adamic--Adar) reaches 0.5912 under the
corrected protocol -- within 3.6 points of the trained heterogeneous graph transformer
(0.6276). Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines} gives all four heuristics against both
negative regimes.
\item Under the conventional protocol, a heuristic that ignores the miRNA entirely
(gene in-degree, 0.8712) beats the trained model (0.8056).
\item A model trained with uniform negatives and then scored against degree-matched
negatives collapses toward chance (0.5352 $\pm$ 0.0202 on \texttt{graphs\_v3fixed},
4 seeds; a single-seed pre-fix-graph measurement gave 0.5118, indistinguishable from
the gene-degree heuristic's 0.5126) -- direct mechanistic evidence that
uniform-negative training teaches a popularity shortcut, not target specificity.
Figure~\ref{fig:negative_sampler_collapse} shows this mismatch collapse holds across
every trained architecture, not just the HGT.

\subsection*{The inflation generalizes across architectures}
\begin{itemize}
\item Six architectures (heterogeneous graph transformer, homogeneous GCN, a
co-expression ablation, a graph-free MLP, an untrained random-weight control, and a
no-miRNA-input ablation) run through the full 2$\times$2 protocol grid.
\item Every trained architecture inflates 0.24--0.31 between the conventional and
corrected protocols, including the graph-free MLP. The untrained control does not move
($-0.004$). Reported as $n=1$ (seed 42) by design -- inflation magnitude dwarfs the
0.005--0.02 seed spread measured elsewhere, but this is not a claim requiring error
bars to be legible.
\item Secondary finding: a plain homogeneous GCN (0.6236) outperforms the heterogeneous
transformer (0.6080) under the corrected protocol -- the architecture the project was
built around is not earning its added complexity even against the simplest graph
baseline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A control task that survives its own audit}
\begin{itemize}
\item Cell-type classification, from the same graph and pipeline: 0.9916 test accuracy.
Labels are the argmax of marker-gene scores computed on expression; the model's only
cell feature is a PCA of that same expression matrix -- a structurally similar
circularity risk to the miRNA-target task's own dependence on miRDB.
\item Two no-graph controls on the raw PCA features (nearest-centroid, logistic
regression) reach 0.4654 and 0.6692 respectively -- a 32-point gap to 0.9916, an order
of magnitude larger than the 3.6-point gap in the link-prediction task. This result is
not circular; the graph is doing substantial, non-trivial work here.
\item Included specifically to demonstrate the audit is not indiscriminate: the same
methodology that collapsed the link-prediction headline leaves this one standing.
Table~\ref{tab:celltype_control} gives both controls against the HGT reference.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{[STAGE, optional] Architectural limits on context-specific claims}
\begin{itemize}
\item Brief, generalizable methodological note (not a headline result): any claim of
context-specific interaction prediction (e.g., cell-type-specific regulation) should be
checked against three questions -- is the context variable in the training artifact, does
the scoring head receive it, and does the reported evaluation vary with it. Framed
generically per decision D2 (goal.md \S3.2); no disease-specific narrative attached
(Figure~\ref{fig:target_predictor}).
\end{itemize}

architecture, e.g.\ $\mathrm{score}(m,g,c)$, could.}
\label{fig:target_predictor}
\end{figure}

\section*{Discussion}

Each half of the inflation we measure already has a name in the broader link-prediction
literature, and neither is specific to microRNAs. The edge-split half is an instance of
what \cite{zhu2023pitfalls} call \emph{target-link inclusion} -- the target edge remains
reachable to the encoder through message passing at evaluation time, so the model can, in
effect, recover the label from the neighborhood it was never supposed to have. Its
lineage traces to the WN18/FB15k inverse-relation leak documented by
\cite{toutanova2015observed}, which was serious enough to motivate a replacement
benchmark (FB15k-237) rather than a footnote; Figure~\ref{fig:edge_split} is the same
mechanism restated for a bipartite miRNA--gene graph with explicit reverse relations
in place of duplicated inverse triples. The negative-sampling half is \emph{degree bias}
(also \emph{popularity bias}): \cite{aiyappa2024implicit} name and quantify it generally,
and \cite{yilmaz2025biasaware} document the identical pattern in network biology
specifically, the domain this graph is drawn from. Mechanistically, degree bias is a
textbook case of \emph{shortcut learning} \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}: a model trained
under uniform negatives has no incentive to learn miRNA-specific regulatory signal when
gene in-degree alone -- a feature that does not even require knowing which miRNA is
being scored -- separates positives from negatives almost for free
(Figure~\ref{fig:neg_sampling_mechanism}); collapsing to chance the moment that shortcut
is closed off (Table~\ref{tab:model_free_baselines}) is the signature of a model that
learned the shortcut and nothing else, not of a harder evaluation. The historical root of
treating unlabeled pairs as negatives at all traces to sampled-metric evaluation practice
in recommender systems \cite{krichene2020sampled}, a convention that migrated into link
prediction without a matching re-derivation of when it is and is not valid.

What we have not found named in the literature is the interaction between the two: every
prior treatment we are aware of studies target-link inclusion or degree bias in
isolation. Table~\ref{tab:headline_grid} and Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_grid} show they are
super-additive on this graph -- correcting either alone leaves the number substantially
inflated, and the cross-architecture grid (Table~\ref{tab:architecture_grid},
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_inflation}) shows the same compounding recurs across six
independently-trained architectures, including a graph-free MLP, with the untrained
control the only one that does not move. We propose calling this \emph{compounded
evaluation inflation} to distinguish it from either pitfall reported alone: the
practical implication is that auditing a paper for target-link inclusion \emph{or} for a
model-free baseline, but not both, can still miss a substantially inflated headline
number.

\begin{itemize}
\item [STAGE] Limitations: $n=1$ for the cross-architecture grid (justify via effect
size vs. seed noise, already established); the task's information ceiling is bounded by
the graph carrying no sequence information while the label source (miRDB) is itself a
seed-complementarity model -- discuss what would and would not fix this (predicting
\emph{experimentally validated} interactions, e.g. miRTarBase, instead of another
predictor's output).
\item [STAGE] Actionable recommendation for reviewers/editors in this subfield: require
(a) an automated leak-free split check, (b) at least one model-free baseline, (c)
matched train/eval negative distributions, as a minimum reporting bar.
\end{itemize}
